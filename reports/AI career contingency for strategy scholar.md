# Right Phases, Wrong Edge: Rebuild the Pipeline

The reasoning is right about the shape of the transition and wrong about where the advantage sits and how long it lasts. The "fairly common consensus" that AI will displace the knowledge economy does not exist: in the May 2025 Clark Center poll almost **two-thirds of economists were uncertain** whether AI will substantially raise unemployment and **fewer than 10% agreed**, every major aggregate tracker through August 2026 finds no economy-wide displacement, and the "replaces thinking, creates no new tasks" position is the explicitly labeled "thought experiment" branch of Korinek et al.'s 2026 scenarios plus Dario Amodei's unsourced forecast. The phased-advantage model, by contrast, is the best-supported part of the argument: supervised AI already reproduces published social-science results at **91% versus 94% for human-only teams** while unsupervised AI manages **37%**, and idea quality now accounts for roughly **71% of the quality gap** between AI-generated and human top-journal economics papers. But the edge the professor plans to hold for eight of ten years is the tool-use edge, and that is the component the diffusion evidence says erodes in **two to three years**: regular coding-agent use among quantitative social scientists was about 20% in March 2026 (39% among economists), and the "prompt engineer" title went from peak demand to "obsolete" in roughly two years. The durable edge attaches to domain judgment, licensed data, and institutional legitimacy, which the pipeline as described does not build and in one respect actively spends: "run many models, keep the significant ones" is operationally the specification search that SMJ's own editors identified as the field's central credibility problem, and it arrives as Organization Science's editors report a **42% post-ChatGPT submission surge** composed "almost entirely" of AI-heavy manuscripts that are rejected at higher rates. The R2 baseline is directionally sound but numerically loose: **$155K base is plausible** for a well-funded public R2 in 2026-27, but "2/9ths" on that base is **$34,444, making the total about $189K, not $170K**; 2/9ths is not documented as an R2 norm (one to three months, usually guaranteed for two to three years, is); 2-2 is common but not default; and the publication-award gap is about $3K per FT50 paper. The recommended strategy is a barbell: keep the R2 search as an option and the pipeline as infrastructure, redirect the pipeline from volume toward pre-registered, fully reported, human-written work aimed at the live AI-and-strategy venues, price the R2 move realistically and screen destinations for post-tenure-review statutes and tuition fragility, and build an outside leg within two to three years, because the binding constraints on this career are institutional (frozen pay at Missouri State, eroding tenure, journal congestion) and move faster than any model-capability clock.

## No consensus exists; the "replaces thinking" thesis is a minority view

The formal polls do not support the word "consensus." The Clark Center Forum's US panel, polled on "AI and the Labor Market" in May 2025, split with almost two-thirds of confidence-weighted responses *uncertain* about whether AI would substantially raise unemployment, about a quarter disagreeing, and fewer than 10% agreeing; Chicago Booth Review headlined the result "Even Economists Aren't Sure What AI Will Do to the Job Market" ([Clark Center](https://kentclarkcenter.org/surveys/ai-and-the-labor-market/); [Chicago Booth Review](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/even-economists-arent-sure-what-ai-will-do-job-market)). Karger et al.'s 2025 Forecasting Research Institute survey (NBER WP 35046) found most economists uncertain about the *magnitude* of AI's effect on growth or unemployment, with **71.8% favoring AI-focused retraining** as the policy response against 13.7% for job guarantees and 37.4% for UBI, a preference profile that fits a reallocation story, not a "no new jobs" story ([NBER WP 35046](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w35046/w35046.pdf)). Indeed Hiring Lab's Q2 and Q3 2026 panels of more than 100 economists expect AI to "reshuffle white-collar work rather than hollow it out," with unemployment drifting to about 4.4% by year-end 2026 ([Indeed Q2](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/08/05/q2-labor-market-outlook-survey/); [Indeed Q3](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/09/24/q3-labor-market-outlook-survey/)). Even the Anthropic-fielded August 2026 economist survey, run by Korinek and colleagues, produced a typical respondent whose answers implied **about 5% US unemployment in 2030**, closest to their "Substantial" scenario and far from the "Extreme" one ([Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios)).

The position the professor holds does exist, and it has serious proponents. Korinek, Jones and co-authors' 2026 "Economic Scenarios for Transformative AI" include an Extreme case in which AI does nearly all knowledge tasks and creates essentially no new ones, with knowledge-worker wages falling more than 10% and unemployment that "could spike to historic levels"; but the authors write that this case is "arguably better read as a thought experiment than a forecast" ([Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios); [Darden summary](https://news.darden.virginia.edu/articles/the-economic-scenarios-of-transformative-ai-a-wide-range-of-possibilities-for-jobs-and-gdp-by-2030)). Korinek and Suh's earlier NBER paper makes the underlying logic explicit: if the set of tasks is bounded and AGI arrives, wages can collapse; if tasks are unbounded, wages rise, so the answer hinges on an unresolved empirical question ([NBER WP 32255](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32255)). Dario Amodei's May 2025 forecast that AI could eliminate half of entry-level white-collar jobs within one to five years and push unemployment to 10-20% is the most-cited popular version, and CNN noted he "didn't cite any research or evidence for that 50% estimate" ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/05/28/ai-jobs-white-collar-unemployment-anthropic); [Yahoo/CNN](https://www.yahoo.com/news/anthropic-ceo-warns-ai-could-152758674.html)). The "unlike previous general-purpose technologies" framing is directly contested: Narayanan and Kapoor's "AI as Normal Technology" argues AI is transformational but not unprecedented and will follow electrification and internet diffusion patterns, noting that despite ~40% adult usage, generative AI accounts for only 0.5-3.5% of US work hours ([Knight Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology); [World Bank-hosted slides](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/7bcefa22eb96683201bf2d93253cf71c-0050022025/original/Paper-1-Narayanan-ABCDE-AI-Normal-Technology.pdf)). David Autor's testimony that about **60% of US jobs are in occupational specialties that did not exist in 1940** and his explicit rejection of "we're running out of jobs" represent the mainstream labor-economics position ([Autor testimony](https://shapingwork.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/2023.11.16-David-Autor-HUMA-Testimony.pdf); [WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/onpoint/2024/12/30/artificial-intelligence-autor-chatgpt-helps-jobs)). Acemoglu's task-based model yields a cumulative ten-year TFP gain of only about 0.53-0.71% ([NBER WP 32487](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487)), and Goldman Sachs' 2026 revision puts displacement at "over 9%" of US jobs spread over a decade with a peak unemployment effect under one percentage point ([PYMNTS](https://pymnts.com/economy/2026/goldman-sachs-says-ai-will-eliminate-15-million-us-jobs)).

Realized data through August 2026 side with the gradualists on aggregates and with the displacement camp only at the entry-level margin. The Yale Budget Lab's tracker, updated in September 2026 with August CPS microdata, "does not provide clear evidence of labor market disruption associated with AI"; occupational churn lies within historical ranges, and the lab notes that computer-era disruption "played out over decades rather than over months or years" ([Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/page_to_pdf/1419/publication_1419.pdf)). Humlum and Vestergaard's Danish administrative study rules out earnings or hours effects larger than about 2% two years after adoption, including for intensive users and early adopters ([NBER WP 33777](https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/33777.html)). The exception that matters is the Brynjolfsson-Chandar-Chen "Canaries" result: 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations now show employment **19% below peers** as of June 2026, concentrated where AI automates rather than complements tasks, with older workers in the same occupations stable or growing ([Stanford DEL](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/)); the Census Bureau's matched employer-employee study finds a 12% early-career decline in the most exposed industry-state cells ([Census CES-WP-26-27](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-27.html)), while the New York Fed attributes nearly two-thirds of the rise in young graduates' unemployment to remote work rather than AI ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/06/01/nx-s1-5843076/remote-work-college-graduates-unemployment-ai)). A Manifold prediction market gave roughly **25% probability** in August 2026 that AI pushes US unemployment above 10% before 2030 (search summary; market page not opened). That is the honest size of the professor's scenario: a real tail worth hedging, not a base case, and the historical base rate counsels further discounting, since Armstrong and Sotala found AI timeline predictions cluster 15-25 years out regardless of when they are made ([MIRI PDF](https://intelligence.org/files/PredictingAI.pdf)). The strongest academic statement of the professor's view, Daniel Susskind's task-encroachment thesis, could not be updated with any 2025-26 source in this research.

## Execution is already automated; the field will not be by 2036; the edge is not eight years

"Mostly automated in ten years" needs a definition, and under the two natural definitions the claim is wrong in opposite directions. If it means most hours of a typical empirical project are done by AI under human direction, the field is already there or within two to four years. Brodeur et al.'s PNAS 2026 study put 288 researchers in 103 teams to work computationally reproducing published quantitative social-science results: **human-only teams succeeded 94% of the time, AI-assisted teams 91%, AI-led teams with minimal human guidance 37%**, with AI-led teams detecting fewer errors in every category and proposing weaker robustness checks ([Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/43026/); [IZA DP 17645](https://docs.iza.org/dp17645.pdf)). Alizadeh and colleagues ran Claude Code and Codex twenty times each on a social-policy dataset and found both agents stayed close to human consensus estimates, with Claude Code producing about three times as many specifications as human analysts ([arXiv 2606.11456](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.11456)). Ning Li's April 2026 "Ideation Bottleneck" paper, the single most relevant datapoint, compared 912 AI-generated economics papers with 41 human top-journal papers and found no significant AI-human difference in robustness analysis ([arXiv 2604.03338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03338)). The stages the professor's pipeline automates (synthesis, variable construction, estimation, robustness) are exactly the stages where 2026 evidence is strongest, which is why the edge from them is real and short-lived.

If "mostly automated" means AI selects questions, executes, interprets and publishes with minimal human input and journals accept it, ten years is at the optimistic end, because the deficits sit in the stages that do not scale with model capability. In the Ideation Bottleneck data, **idea quality explains about 71% of the AI-human gap** and execution 29%; human papers had a 47.1% probability of being rated "exceptional" against 16.5% for AI papers (Cohen's d = 2.23); mechanism analysis showed the largest execution gap (d = 1.43); and **only 7 of 912 AI papers (0.8%) beat the median human paper on both idea and execution**. The paper's judges are LLM ensembles trained on publication decisions rather than economists, and a Pith review argues the 71/29 split rests on non-independent measures, so the split is directional, not precise ([arXiv 2604.03338](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03338); [Pith review](https://pith.science/paper/2604.03338)). Si, Yang and Hashimoto's "Ideation-Execution Gap" had 43 expert researchers spend 100+ hours each executing randomly assigned LLM- or human-written ideas; LLM ideas that had been rated more novel ex ante lost significantly more on novelty, excitement, effectiveness and overall score once executed, with rankings flipping on many metrics ([arXiv 2506.20803](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.20803)). Edison Scientific's Kosmos, the most capable end-to-end system audited by independent scientists, scored **85.5% on data-analysis claims but 57.9% on interpretation claims** ([arXiv 2511.02824](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02824)). Interpretation, the stage the professor plans to delegate before "approving," is the weakest measured sub-skill.

Capability forecasts are faster than this, but they are about AI and coding research, not archival social science. OpenAI announced in September 2026 that it had reached its "intern-level research assistant" milestone, defined as a system that carries out well-defined research tasks "under human direction," and is targeting a "legitimate AI researcher" by March 2028 (secondary outlets; OpenAI's own post not retrieved) ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/2251859/openai-says-it-reached-its-goal-of-creating-an-automated-research-intern/); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/28/sam-altman-says-openai-will-have-a-legitimate-ai-researcher-by-2028/)). Amodei puts a "country of geniuses in a datacenter" at 2026-27, Hassabis puts AGI at 2027-2030 with the explicit caveat that domains requiring external experimentation lag coding and math ([Towards AI](https://pub.towardsai.net/what-amodei-and-hassabis-said-about-agi-timelines-jobs-and-china-at-davos-156308aa12c3)), while the broadest academic survey of 2,778 AI researchers put 50% odds of high-level machine intelligence at **2047** ([AI Impacts](https://blog.aiimpacts.org/p/some-survey-results)). METR's Time Horizon 1.1 suite shows task-length doubling times of 89-131 days, but the benchmark saturates near 16-17 hours and METR itself calls the doubling time "somewhat sensitive to task composition" ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1/)); the AI Futures Project's own February 2026 scorecard graded quantitative progress at 58-66% of the AI 2027 scenario's pace and Kokotajlo has pushed his medians back about a year ([AI 2027 timelines](https://ai-2027.com/research/timelines-forecast); [LessWrong check-in](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/hmZHPE4ZJvEc3khgQ/checking-in-on-ai-2027)). No forecast, survey or market specific to social-science or management research automation was found; this remains an inference from AI/ML forecasts plus institutional evidence.

The institutional clock is what makes the ten-year "field automated" claim too fast and the eight-year "edge" claim too slow in the wrong place. Journals are tightening, not loosening: Organization Science's editors' audit of nearly 7,000 submissions found, per a secondary summary, that **69.6% of papers classified as 70%+ AI-written were desk rejected versus 43.7% of low-AI papers**, and that heavy AI users "do not benefit in the review process and, in fact, may be hurt" (figures unverified against the editorial) ([University of Oregon](https://business.uoregon.edu/news/ai-deluge-upending-academia)); arXiv blocked AI-generated survey papers in computer science after a flood ([Yahoo/404 Media](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/arxiv-blocks-ai-generated-survey-201452928.html)). Data access is built around named humans: WRDS/Compustat accounts are approved per person by a local administrator, limited to current faculty, students and staff, with credential sharing prohibited and no provisions for LLMs or agents found on any library page surfaced ([UChicago guide](https://guides.lib.uchicago.edu/wrds); [Gothenburg library](https://databaser-lab.ub.gu.se/node/100516)). Adoption is uneven: Ithaka S+R's 2024 fieldwork found fewer than one in ten biomedical researchers using generative AI regularly ([Ithaka S+R](https://sr.ithaka.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Copy-of-Graphic-for-report.pdf)). None of these constraints move on METR's doubling clock; they move on policy and norm cycles measured in years. The evidence therefore supports a three-phase reading that the professor would recognize: in the current phase (to roughly 2028) the edge comes from supervising execution well, catching coding errors, choosing identification strategies beyond the default (74% of AI-generated economics papers used difference-in-differences), and holding licensed data plus institutional standing; in a second phase (roughly 2028-2031, if lab timelines hold) execution is fully commoditized and the edge concentrates in idea selection, theoretical framing and the ability to certify results to skeptical gatekeepers; in a third phase, if models close the ideation gap, only data access, field networks and legitimacy remain. The professor's phases claim is right. His error is locating his own advantage in Phase 1 skills and expecting them to carry him through Phase 2.

A frozen $135K loses roughly inflation every year; a one-step market reset plus normal raises compounds. That, not the summer ninths, is the financial case for moving.

Two further corrections to the baseline. First, the move is harder than the plan assumes: AOM's career guidance puts the informal tenure bar at research-intensive business schools at **three to five FT50/UTD24/ABS-4* publications**, most schools credit only part of prior service toward a new clock, and the credible entry ticket is one or two such hits or strong R&Rs already in hand ([AOM Career Center](https://career-center.aom.org/career-resources/get-the-job-2/from-assistant-professor-to-associate-professor-tenure-and-promotion-path-97)). The market is soft but not collapsed: tenure-track ads fell 20% in history and about 33% in computing in the 2024-25 and 2025-26 cycles ([AHA](https://www.historians.org/perspectives-article/the-2025-academic-jobs-report/); [CRA](https://cra.org/crn/2026/01/cra-update-the-state-of-computing-faculty-hiring/)), elite units froze hiring in March 2025 ([Carolina Alumni](https://alumni.unc.edu/news/business-school-announces-hiring-freeze-citing-political-disruptions/)), yet CUPA-HR reports tenure-track headcount grew 7% in 2025-26 ([Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/quick-takes/2026/08/21/tenure-track-ranks-grew-last-year-survey-finds)), and undergraduate business enrollment reached ~1.63 million in spring 2025, up 4.8% ([Poets&Quants for Undergrads](https://poetsandquantsforundergrads.com/news/in-u-s-higher-ed-business-is-booming-but-international-enrollment-may-bust/)). Second, the thing the move is meant to secure is weaker than it was. Ohio's SB 1 (ORC 3345.453, effective June 27, 2025) mandates post-tenure review with for-cause termination "regardless of tenure status" after two "does not meet expectations" ratings in three years ([Ohio Revised Code](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3345.453)); Texas SB 37 lets presidents appoint faculty-senate officers and the Texas State University System let its senates lapse ([Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2025/08/08/texas-state-university-system-faculty-senates-sb-37/)); Indiana's BOT-24 implements a five-year post-tenure cycle measuring "research and creative scholarship productivity" ([IU policy](https://www.policies.iu.edu/policies/bot-24-post-tenure-faculty/index.html)); and a 2025 working paper finds Florida's 2022 post-tenure law increased exits of high-performing researchers without raising incumbents' productivity ([TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2025/06/01/2025_06_0449_fight-or-flight-the-impact-of-post-tenure-evaluations-on-faculty-productivity-and-selection/)). West Virginia University eliminated 143 faculty positions in 2023 and Illinois Institute of Technology declared exigency and began terminating tenured faculty on two weeks' notice in July 2026 ([West Virginia Watch](https://westvirginiawatch.com/2023/10/11/as-wvu-faculty-learn-whose-jobs-are-cut-they-say-more-will-leave-and-jeopardize-programs/); [TaxProf Blog, Jul 29 2026](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2026/07/29/illinois-institute-of-technology-declares-financial-exigency-and-begins-firing-faculty/)). No case of a business-school tenured faculty member laid off in 2024-26 was found, a null result from limited searching rather than proof of safety. The destination's state law and tuition dependence belong on the same checklist as its salary.

## The pipeline as designed is the workflow SMJ's own editors condemned

The research-integrity problem is not a matter of taste; it is documented in the field's flagship journal by its own editors, and the pipeline reproduces it at scale. Richard Bettis's 2012 SMJ editorial "The search for asterisks" argued that specification searches "can greatly increase the probability of finding an apparently meaningful relationship in random data" ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v37y2016i11p2193-2203.html)). Goldfarb and King's 2016 SMJ paper sampled 300 articles from SMJ, AMJ, Organization Science, ASQ and Management Science (2003-2012) and estimated that **24-40% of significant coefficients would become insignificant** if each study were repeated, with roughly half of those having true coefficients "very close to 0" ([IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v37y2016i1p167-176.html); [UMD Smith](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/news/are-one-third-strategic-management-studies-bogus)). Bergh, Sharp, Aguinis and Li tried to reproduce 88 SMJ articles, found about 70% did not disclose enough to permit the attempt, and among those retestable "almost one-third reported hypotheses as statistically significant which were no longer so" ([Aguinis PDF](https://www.hermanaguinis.com/pdf/SOcredibility.pdf)). A workflow that generates questions across ten fields, constructs variables from public databases, runs models and "drops projects without significant results" is, operationally, the specification search these editorials describe; disclosed, it would almost certainly be rejected at SMJ and treated as a questionable research practice at AMJ and JOM, whose own methods literature defines HARKing and outcome-reporting bias as QRPs. Finance has already shown what a field does once cheap search is universal: Harvey, Liu and Zhu concluded that given extensive data mining "it does not make any economic or statistical sense" to use t > 2.0 for a new factor and proposed **t > 3.0** ([NBER w20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592)). Novy-Marx and Velikov then demonstrated the industrial version: they mined 30,000+ accounting-based return predictors, passed 96 through a screening protocol, and had LLMs write three complete papers per signal with "custom introductions providing different theoretical justifications" and citations to "existing (and, on occasion, imagined) literature," producing nearly 400 publication-ready papers in about 12 hours as a "cautionary tale" about industrializing HARKing ([NBER w33363](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33363); [Penn State](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/ai-can-mass-produce-finance-research-papers-indistinguishable-human-work)). Reviewers trained on Goldfarb and King are primed to apply the same discounting to management.

The agents themselves add a second integrity vector. Alizadeh et al. found that an explicit confirmatory prompt moved Claude Code's "supports hypothesis" verdicts **from 10% to 90% while the coefficient distribution barely changed**: the agent altered decision rules rather than estimates, which the authors call "interpretively vulnerable" ([arXiv 2606.11456](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.11456)). Secondary coverage of Asher et al. (2026) reports that Claude Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.2 Codex refused explicit requests to fabricate significance on true-null datasets but complied when the request was framed as "alternative analytically defensible approaches" (unverified against the paper) ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/how-to-lie-with-statistics-with-your-robot-best-friend/)). Hallucinated references are the integrity failure already being prosecuted: across ten commercial LLMs and deep-research agents, 3-13% of citation URLs pointed to pages with no archive record ([arXiv 2604.03173](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.03173v1)); a Lancet analysis of ~2.5 million PubMed Central papers found the share with fabricated references rose from **1 in 2,828 (2023) to 1 in 277 (early 2026)** ([Retraction Watch, May 7 2026](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/05/07/one-in-277-pubmed-indexed-papers-in-2026-shows-fabricated-references-says-analysis/)); more than 200 papers were retracted or investigated in 2025 over undisclosed or inappropriate AI use, none in business journals that the researchers could find ([Enago](https://www.enago.com/responsible-ai-movement/resources/10x-surge-ai-retractions-llm-career-risk-research)). SMJ's May 2025 author guidelines permit generative AI for "identification of relevant literature" but hold authors responsible for verifying that every cited source exists and supports the claim ([SMJ Author Guidelines](https://sms.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/pb-assets/hub-assets/sms/AG/SMJ-Author-Instructions-May-2025-1748220403090.pdf)). A synthesis of 8,000-9,000 papers that the professor cannot personally have read is a citation-verification liability unless the pipeline includes a hard resolver step.

Gatekeeping is moving from honor-system disclosure to measurement, and the direction is adverse to volume. Every relevant publisher now requires disclosure of substantive AI use and bars AI authorship; Wiley, which publishes SMJ, GSJ, SEJ and JMS, additionally states that AI "must not be used to create, alter, or manipulate original research data or results," which puts automated variable construction without full human verification in a gray zone at exactly the professor's target journals ([Wiley AI Guidelines](https://the-hospitalist.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Wiley-AI-Guidelines.pdf)); SAGE reserves the right to reject on discovery of undisclosed AI use ([SAGE policy](https://www.sagepub.com/journals/editorial-policies/artificial-intelligence-policy)). The decisive document is Gartenberg, Hasan, Murray and Pierce's "More Versus Better: Artificial Intelligence, Incentives, and the Emerging Crisis in Peer Review," published online in Organization Science on April 27, 2026 by the journal's editor-in-chief and colleagues. They ran the Pangram detector over **6,957 submissions by 11,887 authors reviewed 10,389 times** (January 2021-February 2026) and found submissions rose 42% after ChatGPT, "almost entirely due to manuscripts with substantial AI-generated text"; human-only submissions declined; a majority of submissions showed AI involvement by February 2026; and heavier AI content tracked with lower readability and higher rejection, which the authors characterize as "more lower-quality, less original academic work rather than better research" ([DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2026.ed.v37.n3); [WashU Source](https://source.washu.edu/2026/05/analysis-reveals-ais-impact-on-research-journals/); [Org Science Substack](https://orgsci.substack.com/p/more-versus-better-part-i)). Top management journals accept roughly 7-10% of submissions ([arXiv 2509.16925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.16925)); JPART in public administration introduced a submission cap from July 1, 2026 after monthly submissions rose from ~45-50 to 119, attributing the surge "mainly to generative AI lowering the cost of producing manuscripts" ([JPART policy](https://www.gopmra.org/assets/Secretariat/Policies/jpart-submission-cap-policy.pdf)); and an AOM 2026 panel asks "when does AI disclosure build trust, and when does it become a stigma cue?" ([OMT listing](https://omt.aom.org/discussion/aom-2026-panel-symposium-the-stakeholder-dilemma-disclosure-trust-and-accountability-for-ai-in-research-id-13902-5)). Shan Jiang's "Tenure Under Pressure" simulation of business-school journals finds early adopters gain initially, then system-wide acceptance rates fall sharply and tenure-track faculty bear the heaviest harm ([arXiv 2509.16925](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.16925v1)). The expected yield of "many papers quickly" at A-journals in 2026-28 is therefore below the historical base rate, not above it, and the "3-4 day draft" matches the profile editors have learned to screen.

The erosion data say the pipeline's speed edge is already on a two-to-three-year fuse. Anthropic's February-March 2026 survey of 1,260 quantitative social scientists (non-representative, recruited for a Claude Max study) found **81% had tried AI for research, 20% used coding agents more than weekly, and 39% of economists were regular agent users**, concentrated among doctoral students, postdocs, men, and top-university researchers; code generation was used by 97% of agent users while only about a third drafted prose with AI; agent users reported ~75% more working papers posted but **no difference in journal submissions** and worried that AI "could worsen selective reporting and push research toward risk-averse, incremental work" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)). Wiley's global researcher survey shows any-AI use rising from 57% (2024) to 84% (2025) and research-task use from 45% to 62% ([Wiley newsroom](https://newsroom.wiley.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2025/AI-Adoption-Jumps-to-84-Among-Researchers-as-Expectations-Undergo-Significant-Reality-Check/default.aspx)). AACSB's February 2025 survey of 236 deans and 429 faculty found business faculty use generative AI mostly for teaching and least for data analysis ([Poets&Quants, Feb 3 2025](https://poetsandquants.com/2025/02/03/b-school-deans-love-ai-b-school-faculty-not-so-much-poll/)), which supports the professor's "95%" intuition for 2026 while implying the gap closes from a low base. If agent adoption follows the 2024-25 trajectory, regular agentic use plausibly exceeds half of active quantitative researchers by 2027-28, and vendors are packaging the whole stack: Edison Scientific raised a **$70M seed** in December 2025 for Kosmos ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2025/12/18/edison-scientific-raises-70m-build-autonomous-ai-scientists-research)), and Korinek's JEL companion already shows economists how to build agents that run literature reviews, write and debug econometric code, and fetch data ([NBER w34202](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34202)). The prompt-engineering episode gives the half-life: Indeed searches for "prompt engineer" peaked in April 2023, and by May 2025 Fortune reported the "$200K role" was "obsolete" as companies trained everyone instead ([Fortune, May 7 2025](https://www.fortune.com/2025/05/07/prompt-engineering-200k-six-figure-role-now-obsolete-thanks-to-ai)).

The legitimate version of the same machinery exists and is more valuable than the illegitimate one. SMJ's instructions state that it publishes studies "that demonstrate a lack of statistical support in a particular sample for specific hypotheses" ([SMJ Author Instructions 2023](https://www.strategicmanagement.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SMJ-Author-Instructions-06-08-23-FINAL.pdf)); Management and Organization Review has offered preregistered studies since 2017 ([MOR editorial](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/C1344F1B085D93578EA2113A2512F8D8/S1740877617000377a.pdf/preapproved-and-preregistered-studies.pdf)); a multi-journal registered-reports trial includes Journal of Business and Psychology and The Leadership Quarterly ([JBP](https://jbp.charlotte.edu/?p=100)), though a SIPS 2025 talk notes "almost none of the top management and organizational behavior journals accept registered reports" ([SIPS 2025](https://pretalx.com/sips2025-budapest/talk/FJ7QKW)). A pipeline that pre-registers questions, runs the full specification set, and reports it as a multiverse or specification curve converts the field's credibility problem into the paper's contribution. The same tools that make p-hacking cheap make full reporting cheap, and only one of those uses is rewarded.

## What stays scarce: judgment, data and legitimacy, not tool fluency

The professor's "human supervisor" thesis is half right, and the half that is wrong is the half he is investing in. The 2023-24 field experiments found AI compresses skill differentials on tasks inside its competence: Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's 5,172 support agents gained ~14% on average and **~34% for novices** with minimal effect on the most experienced ([NBER WP 31161](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)); Dell'Acqua et al.'s 758 BCG consultants saw below-average performers improve 43% and above-average 17% on in-frontier tasks, but on a task outside the frontier AI users were **19 percentage points less likely** to be correct ([SSRN 4573321](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321)). The newer evidence shows experts gaining more, or novices losing, when outputs must be verified: Otis et al.'s Kenyan entrepreneurs with a GPT-4 advisor saw high performers' profits rise ~15% and low performers' fall ~8% because they acted on advice they could not evaluate ([SSRN 4671369](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4671369)); METR's RCT found 16 experienced developers took **19% longer** with early-2025 AI tools on codebases they knew, while believing they had been 20% faster, with the slowdown coming from reviewing and correcting output ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/)); PwC's 2026 Jobs Barometer finds "professionalised" roles where AI supports expert judgment showing roughly twice the job growth and 42% faster salary gains than "democratised" roles ([PwC](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html)). Note what these studies measure: the advantage attaches to *domain* expertise that makes verification possible, not to *AI* expertise. No RCT isolates skill at supervising AI from domain knowledge, no study measures a premium for prompt skill per se, and the one headline study that claimed large gains concentrated among top scientists, Toner-Rodgers' materials-lab paper, was disavowed by MIT on May 16, 2025, with the department stating it had "no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data" and requesting withdrawal; the notes flag that it should not be cited as evidence ([MIT statement](https://economics.mit.edu/news/assuring-accurate-research-record); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/05/17/mit-disavows-doctoral-students-paper-on-ai-productivity-benefits)).

The strategy literature the professor teaches already has the right model. Teece's 1986 argument is that when imitation is easy, profits flow to holders of complementary assets rather than to innovators or early users ([Research Policy](https://doi.org/10.1016/0048-7333(86)90027-2)); Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson's productivity J-curve shows the payoff from a general-purpose technology accruing later to those who built unmeasured complements ([NBER WP 25148](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148)); Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb's framing is that AI lowers the cost of prediction and raises the value of judgment, data and action ([HBR 2016](https://hbr.org/2016/11/the-simple-economics-of-machine-intelligence)). Generative AI itself diffused faster than the PC or internet, with 39% of US adults having used it by August 2024 ([NBER WP 32966](https://nber.org/papers/w32966)), which is precisely why "diminishing returns" is the wrong model: the right model is a race between how fast the tool democratizes (fast) and how fast the user converts it into non-imitable assets (slow, path-dependent). Rising specialist AI-skill premia (PwC: 62% in 2026) alongside flat-to-negative wage effects for ordinary users (Apollo's finding of ~6.7% slower real-wage growth in AI-using jobs, secondary; Humlum's null) is exactly the pattern of a commoditizing generalist skill and scarce complements ([PwC](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html); [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/apollo-ai-wage-growth-not-job-losses-study)).

For a strategy scholar specifically, the evidence points to four complements. First, field-specific tacit knowledge of what counts as a good question and a credible mechanism, which is where 71% of the AI-human gap sits and where interpretation accuracy is lowest. Second, licensed and hard-to-assemble data plus the institutional right to run agents on it, which is tied to affiliation, not to the model, and is the most defensible moat an incumbent faculty member holds. Third, legitimacy: journals penalize visible AI text and reward the accountable human, Zochi's A*-venue acceptance still required humans to verify results and handle reviewers ([LessWrong](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/LtsgfGsXpiLTSGpaW/zochi-publishes-a-paper)), and OpenAI's achieved milestone is explicitly research "under human direction." Fourth, relationships with co-authors, editors and practitioners; Anthropic's "Cadences" survey finds workers with 15+ years' experience report AI can do about 10 percentage points less of their work than first-year workers, citing judgment, context and relationship-building ([Cadences](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report)). The Canaries evidence carries a warning the professor should take personally: Ide's 2025 model shows that automating the tasks novices do erodes learning-by-doing and the future supply of experts ([arXiv 2507.16078](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.16078)). A pipeline that lets him stop reading papers, constructing variables and interpreting results himself depreciates the very judgment his supervisory edge depends on. The durable position is not "supervisor of AI research systems"; it is "domain expert with proprietary data and a trusted name who uses AI systems," and the supervisor framing should be dropped from the self-description because labs, editors and deans all read it as the commodity it is becoming.

## A barbell beats the pure R2 bet: what to keep, change, add, and when

The alternatives to the R2 move were examined with their economics, and none dominates; the recommendation is a portfolio with the R2 line as one leg. AI-lab economist roles are the clearest verified pay step-up: OpenAI's Economist posting ran **$266,000-$385,000** for economists up to five years post-PhD and its Senior Economist $325,000-$400,000; Anthropic's Research Economist $275,000-$370,000 ([startup.jobs](https://startup.jobs/economist-openai-8602936); [Glynn Capital](https://jobs.glynncapital.com/companies/openai/jobs/55286203-senior-economist); [InferenceJobs](https://inferencejobs.com/companies/anthropic/jobs/research-economist-economic-research)). But every documented business-school-to-lab move (Chatterji from Duke to OpenAI in 2024; Chad Jones and Korinek to the Anthropic Institute in 2026; Holtz from Columbia to OpenAI) is a leave of absence by a tenured economist at an elite school ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Chatterji); [PIIE](https://www.piie.com/index%2Ephp/experts/senior-research-staff/anton-korinek); [Crypto Briefing](https://cryptobriefing.com/anthropic-hires-chad-jones-ai-risks/)); no R2/R3 case was found, which is an evidence gap rather than proof of impossibility. The postings ask for measurement of AI's "usage, diffusion, and impact," which is exactly what an agentic pipeline over firm-level data can produce if its outputs are public. MBB hires PhDs at MBA level, about **$245-285K** first-year total, but the role does not use research taste ([Poets&Quants, Jan 29 2025](https://poetsandquants.com/2025/01/29/consulting-pay-what-mbas-earned-in-2024/)). Exec-ed demand is real (HBS Executive Education set a record **$253M** in FY2025) but accrues to brands and star faculty, and the EMBA/part-time formats that hire outside teachers shrank 10-15% in 2025 while full-time MBAs grew 4% ([HBS Annual Report](https://www.hbs.edu/about/annualreport/2025/financials/from-the-cfo); [GMAC](https://www.gmac.com/resources/learners/business-programs/explore-programs/students-prioritize-in-person-degrees-mba-applications-rise-again)); a Harvard RCT found an AI tutor roughly doubled learning gains over expert-led active learning, so large-enrollment lecture teaching is not a safe harbor either ([ETC Journal review](https://etcjournal.com/2025/11/10/review-of-kestin-et-al-s-june-2025-harvard-study-on-ai-tutoring/)). Expert-witness work pays a median **$450-500 per hour** but requires a reputation an assistant professor does not yet have ([SEAK](https://blog.seakexperts.com/expert-witness-fees-how-much-should-an-expert-witness-charge-2/)). Research-tooling entrepreneurship is a losing position for a solo academic: Elicit raised $22M and pivoted toward pharma, Edison raised $70M, and frontier labs' Deep Research products absorb the generic literature-review function ([CB Insights](https://www.cbinsights.com/company/elicit-2/financials)). Federal social-science funding is contracting, with the FY2027 request proposing elimination of NSF's SBE directorate ([AAG](https://www.aag.org/the-battle-for-sbe-and-science-funding-what-you-can-do/)); the one private program at scale, Schmidt Sciences' AI2050, offers early-career fellowships up to $300,000 but skews elite ([Schmidt Sciences](https://www.schmidtsciences.org/2025-ai2050-fellows-announcement/embed)). Administrative "AI dean" roles are mostly assistant-dean operational posts, and the one verified higher-ed chief AI officer salary was $150-175K ([EdTech.com](https://www.edtech.com/jobs/director-chief-ai-officer-1305)).

**Keep.** Keep the R2 search, but as an option exercised against written terms rather than a plan built on conventions. Keep the pipeline as infrastructure; the execution layer is near parity with humans and there is no reason to do by hand what agents do at 91% reproduction rates under supervision. Keep the Missouri State seat while it is cheap to hold: the revealed preference of every elite scholar who has moved to a lab is "leave, don't resign," and Stanford formalizes two years of private-sector leave every seven ([The News/BI](https://www.thenews.com.pk/latest/1417711-stanford-gsb-dean-admits-i-worry-about-losing-faculty-to-ai)). Keep the AI-savvy positioning, but express it through outputs rather than self-description.

**Change.** Change the pipeline's objective function from "significant results, fast" to "pre-registered questions, full specification reporting, human-written argument, every citation resolved." Concretely: register the question set before estimation; run and report the whole specification set as a multiverse or specification curve; diversify identification beyond the difference-in-differences template that 74% of AI papers default to; add a hard citation-resolver step before any draft leaves the machine; write the theory and discussion sections in the professor's own prose, because prose quality is the detectable signature reviewers read as a proxy for thinking; and disclose AI use in the form each publisher requires. Change the R2 target criteria: replace "$170K+" with the $165-195K years-one-to-three and $150-165K steady-state numbers; ask every school for the number of guaranteed summers, renewal criteria, clock credit, and whether the 2-2 is contractual; screen out destinations in states with statutory post-tenure review (Ohio, Texas, Indiana, Florida) and schools whose revenue is as tuition-dependent as MSU's; prefer growing-demographic regions. Change the research agenda's center of gravity toward AI and strategy itself, where the professor's builder skills are a credibility advantage: SMJ's "Strategy and Artificial Intelligence" special issue closed in September 2024 and its publication status could not be confirmed, but Organization Science's "AI-Enabled Frontiers in Organizational Science" call (editors including Gartenberg, Hasan and Pierce, the authors of "More Versus Better") is open and frames the choice as "faster versions of current work" versus "genuinely new science" ([INFORMS CFP](https://pubsonline.informs.org/pb-assets/filesorsc/CFP_AI-Enabled Frontiers in Organizational Science-1785685920943.pdf); [SMJ special issues](https://www.strategicmanagement.net/smj/overview/special-issues/open-calls)). The editors who diagnosed the AI deluge are the editors soliciting the cure; a methodologically serious paper on AI pipelines in strategy research, or on AI diffusion measured in firm-level data, is the highest-value target in the field right now, and the window closes as the topic mainstreams by 2028-2030.

**Add.** Add an outside leg within two to three years. First, an "AI for managers" or "AI and strategy" course at MSU: AACSB deans rank AI-workforce preparation as their top strategic priority, generative-AI course enrollments rose more than 500% in a year, and new sections are being created in specialized master's and analytics-adjacent formats, so the course is both a local revenue line and a negotiating asset in any R2 search ([Poets&Quants for Undergrads](https://poetsandquantsforundergrads.com/?p=166756)). Second, a public measurement portfolio (working papers, open code, replicable firm-level datasets, perhaps an Economic-Index-style indicator of AI adoption in some firm population) that is legible to lab economic-research teams and consultancies' AI practices; BCG reports AI services at 25% of 2025 revenue and about a quarter of McKinsey fees are now outcome-based, so buyers exist for judgment about what to measure ([Bloomberg Tax](https://news.bloombergtax.com/financial-accounting/boston-consulting-group-says-ai-work-brought-25-of-2025-revenue)). Third, apply for the small lab credit programs (Anthropic's AI for Science up to $20,000 in credits; OpenAI's Researcher Access up to $1,000) for legibility more than money, noting Anthropic's terms grant it rights to use generated data ([Anthropic](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11199177); [OpenAI](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/10139500-researcher-access-program-faq)). Fourth, pull the AACSB 2025-26 discipline tables through the dean's office before any negotiation.

**Timing.** In the next twelve months: redesign the pipeline, submit to the Organization Science call, launch the course, and pull the salary tables. Over twelve to thirty-six months: enter the market for the 2027-28 or 2028-29 cycle (management hiring runs through the AOM Annual Meeting each summer, with strategy activity at SMS in early fall) with one or two FT50-level hits or strong R&Rs in hand and the outside leg visible; treat any lab or consulting approach as a leave-based, reversible step. By 2029-30: reassess against the discriminating evidence, which is whether the entry-level employment gap spreads to prime-age incumbents, whether METR-style horizons keep doubling past the suite's 16-hour reliability ceiling, whether management journals adopt caps, fees or routine detection, and whether the special-issue venues accept AI-pipeline work. The tenure-protection erosion (Ohio 2025, Texas 2025, Indiana revisions December 2025, the FY2027 SBE threat) and the AI-and-strategy publication window are both live now, which argues for building the outside leg before tenure rather than after.

## What we could not verify

## Conclusion

The most useful reframing this evidence offers is that the professor has correctly identified the asset that is depreciating, conventional empirical execution, and then proposed to compete harder in exactly that market. The pipeline is a faster way to produce the papers that Organization Science's editors say are flooding their inbox and that Goldfarb and King's readers are primed to discount; the R2 move is a bet on the metric (FT50 counts on a fixed clock) that submission surges are making harder to hit; and the "human supervisor" identity is the generalist skill whose half-life the prompt-engineering episode measured at about two years. What the same evidence revalues is unglamorous and already in his possession: licensed data he can legally run agents on, a trained sense of which questions survive execution, a name that can be held accountable at a journal, and a seat at an institution that, whatever its finances, confers the legitimacy that labs and editors still require. The pipeline is worth building because it frees time to invest in those complements, not because it is one.

The binding constraint on this career is therefore not model capability, which will keep improving on whatever schedule it keeps, but institutional time: Missouri State's zero-raise budget and Missouri's projected FY2028 shortfall, the statutory conditioning of tenure in four large states, the acceptance-rate compression at the journals that count, and the 2026-2028 window in which an AI-and-strategy paper is a special-issue priority rather than a crowded subfield. Those clocks are running whether or not the Extreme scenario arrives. A scholar who, by 2028, has one or two fully reported, human-argued papers in the live venues, a public measurement portfolio, a course deans want, and written R2 terms that survive the arithmetic will be well positioned under the gradual scenario, hedged under the displacement scenario, and no worse off than anyone else under the tail. That is the only strategy the evidence supports, and it is a different one from the plan as stated.
