## Key Question 1: Consensus check — is "this wave replaces thinking and will destroy the knowledge economy rather than create new jobs" a consensus, a majority, or a minority view among economists?

### Takeaway
There is no such consensus among economists. The modal position in formal expert polls is *uncertainty*; the dominant position among labor economists who have published on the data is "no aggregate displacement yet, concentrated hiring effects on entry-level workers, outcome depends on adoption and policy." The strong "AI substitutes for cognition itself, so no new work will be created" claim is a minority view among academic economists; it is held most strongly by AI-lab leaders (Amodei), by some forecasters (AI 2027 authors), and as the *extreme* branch of scenario models (Korinek et al. 2026), where the authors themselves call it closer to a thought experiment than a forecast.

### Inferences
- The professor's claim (1) misdescribes the distribution. Counting positions: formal polls show *uncertainty* as the plurality view; the published empiricists (Brynjolfsson, Humlum, Gimbel/Yale, Anthropic economists, Fed researchers, Chen et al.) report no aggregate displacement through mid-2026; the theorists split between "modest" (Acemoglu, Cowen, Narayanan-Kapoor, Autor) and "contingent/scenario" (Korinek, Susskind-type arguments). The "replaces thinking, no new jobs" view maps onto Korinek's *extreme* scenario and Amodei's forecast — a real and respectable position, but a minority one and not an economists' consensus.
- The specific framing "unlike steam/electricity/internet because it substitutes for cognition" is explicitly contested by Narayanan–Kapoor and Autor, and is implicitly contested by the Yale Budget Lab's finding that the current occupational churn rate is within the range seen during the computer and internet transitions (see Q2).
- The gap between lab-leader forecasts (10–20% unemployment within five years) and sell-side/academic forecasts (Goldman: peak unemployment effect <1 pp; Indeed panel: 4.4%) is the single clearest sign that there is no consensus.

## Key Question 2: What has actually happened in knowledge-worker labor markets, 2023–2026?

### Takeaway
Through August 2026 data, every major aggregate tracker (Yale Budget Lab, Brookings, OECD, Anthropic economists, Humlum–Vestergaard in Denmark, Chen et al.) finds no economy-wide displacement or unemployment effect from generative AI; the consistent, growing exception is a relative decline in *hiring and employment of 22–25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations* (now 19% below peers per Stanford's Aug 2026 update), plus measurable losses in narrow, highly automatable task markets (freelance writing/coding, translation). Attribution of the entry-level effect to AI versus remote work, interest rates, and post-pandemic overhiring is actively contested among Federal Reserve researchers.

### Cited Findings

**Aggregate trackers [EMPIRICAL]**
- Yale Budget Lab, "Evaluating the Impact of AI on the Labor Market" (Oct 2025): over the first 33 months after ChatGPT, "the broader labor market has not experienced a discernible disruption"; occupational-mix change is within historical ranges; the lab notes disruption from computers "played out over decades rather than over months or years." Tracker updated Sept 2026 with August 2026 CPS microdata: still "does not provide clear evidence of labor market disruption associated with AI"; occupational churn, AI exposure among the unemployed, and usage data "remain flat, lie within historical ranges, or continue along pre-AI trends." May 2026 note (Ryan Nunn): no clear AI effect yet; the early-2026 market features low layoffs *and* low hiring. Director Martha Gimbel: "No matter which way you look at the data, at this exact moment, it just doesn't seem like there's major macroeconomic effects here." ### Inferences
- The realized pattern to date is "hiring-margin substitution for juniors in automatable task bundles, not firing of incumbents," which is consistent with firms substituting AI for the *most codified* slice of knowledge work. This is closer to Autor's and Brynjolfsson's framing than to "AI replaces thinking."
- Wage evidence is genuinely mixed (Chen/ETLA: exposed-occupation earnings up; Apollo: slower real-wage growth; Humlum: null; Census: slightly slower early-career earnings growth), which argues against confident claims in either direction.
- The entry-level effect matters for the professor's thesis in a different way than he suggests: it is evidence that AI is eroding the *apprenticeship ladder* into expert roles, which is a threat to the long-run supply of the very "skilled human supervisors" he expects to hold the advantage (see Ide 2025 in Q3).

### Gaps
- Could not open the Yale Budget Lab, Stanford, Census, Dallas Fed, or Brookings pages directly; figures come from search-engine summaries of those primary pages.
- No 2026 BLS Employment Projections or official BLS statement specifically attributing occupational changes to AI was found.
- No independent replication of the Canaries result beyond Tucker (2026) was found.

## Key Question 3: Who captures the gains — novices or experts, early or late adopters? Where do "supervision" and "taste" show up as valuable?

### Takeaway
The field-experiment literature of 2023–24 found AI compresses skill differentials *within* a task's "frontier" (novices gain most: +34% for the least-experienced agents in Brynjolfsson–Li–Raymond, +43% for below-average BCG consultants in Dell'Acqua et al.), but the newer evidence (Otis et al. Kenya, METR 2025, Dell'Acqua's outside-the-frontier tasks, 2026 delegation experiments, PwC's "professionalised vs democratised" split, Anthropic's survey) shows experts gain more — or novices actively lose — when tasks are uncertain, when AI output must be *verified*, and when knowing what to ask matters. The one headline study claiming large, inequality-widening gains for top scientists (Toner-Rodgers) was disavowed by MIT in May 2025. The evidence supports "AI-savvy domain experts hold an edge on judgment-heavy work," but it does *not* show that edge is large or durable in routine knowledge work, where leveling dominates.

### Cited Findings

**Evidence that AI levels the field (novices gain most) [EMPIRICAL]**
- Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond (NBER WP 31161, Apr 2023; QJE 2025): 5,172 customer-support agents; generative-AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour ~14% on average, ~34% for novice and low-skilled workers, with minimal effect on the most experienced; mechanism: the tool disseminated top performers' tacit best practices. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [NBER WP 31161](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161)
- Noy & Zhang (Science, July 2023): 453 college-educated professionals doing writing tasks; ChatGPT cut time ~40% and raised quality ~18%; lower-ability writers gained most, so inequality between workers fell. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [Science](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adh2586)
- Dell'Acqua et al., "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier" (HBS WP 24-013, Sept 2023; with Mollick, Lakhani et al.): 758 BCG consultants; on tasks *inside* the frontier, GPT-4 users completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, with 40% higher quality; below-average performers improved 43%, above-average 17%. On a task *outside* the frontier, AI users were 19 pp *less* likely to produce correct answers. The paper coins "centaurs" (divide tasks) and "cyborgs" (integrate continuously). [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [SSRN 4573321](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321)
- Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon & Demirer (Feb 2023): GitHub Copilot users finished a JavaScript server task 55.8% faster (n=95). [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [arXiv 2302.06590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590)

[prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [SSRN 4945566](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4945566)

[verified Oct 2026, secondary] — [Digital Applied review](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/who-gains-most-from-ai-at-work-studies-compared)

### Inferences
- The two bodies of evidence are reconcilable via task type: where the correct output is checkable and the frontier is known (support tickets, routine memos, boilerplate code), AI transfers top performers' tacit practice to novices and *compresses* differentials; where output must be verified and the frontier is jagged (strategy, diagnosis, business advice, mature codebases), domain expertise is the complement and novices can be harmed. "Supervision" and "taste" show up as valuable mainly through the *negative* channel — avoiding confident errors (Dell'Acqua's 19 pp, Otis's −8%, METR's review overhead) — rather than through measured premia for an identifiable "AI supervisor" skill.
- The professor's claim (2) is partly supported — for judgment-heavy work, the person who can evaluate AI output holds the edge — but overstated as a general rule: for most of the knowledge tasks studied in RCTs, AI's main effect has been to make average performance cheaper, which is bad, not good, for anyone whose advantage was above-average execution of codifiable tasks.
- The within-domain advantage appears to attach to *domain* expertise, not to *AI* expertise: no study measured a premium for prompt skill per se, and the only expertise-with-AI data (Anthropic learning curves) is correlational and vendor-produced.

### Gaps
- No RCT isolates "skill at supervising AI" from domain expertise; the "verification judgment" mechanism remains an inference across studies.
- 2026 updates to Brynjolfsson–Li–Raymond, Noy–Zhang, or Dell'Acqua (e.g., with agentic tools) were not found; the Dell'Acqua et al. "Cybernetic Teammate" P&G study (2025) is known to exist but its figures were not verified this session.
- Humlum & Vestergaard's within-firm productivity figures (time savings) could not be re-verified.

## Key Question 4: Erosion dynamics — how long does a tooling advantage last before it is competed away, and what determines the length?

### Takeaway
The GPT literature says the *aggregate* payoff from general-purpose technologies arrives slowly (Paul David: ~40 years for electrification; Brynjolfsson–Rock–Syverson's "productivity J-curve") because complementary organizational and intangible investments take decades — but the *individual tooling* advantage erodes fast, because the tool itself diffuses rapidly (generative AI adoption outpaced the PC and internet) and skill at using it is quickly routinized (the "prompt engineer" title peaked in April 2023 and was declared obsolete by 2025). What persists is not the tool skill but the complementary assets: domain expertise, proprietary data, distribution, institutional position, and tacit knowledge. Direct half-life measurements for AI skill premia do not yet exist; the available job-ad data (PwC: premium *rising* to 62% in 2026) measures *specialist* AI roles, not generalist facility with chat tools.

### Cited Findings
- Bresnahan & Trajtenberg (1995), "General Purpose Technologies: Engines of Growth?": GPTs generate "innovational complementarities" whose realization requires co-invention downstream, making diffusion slow and uneven. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [NBER WP 4148](https://www.nber.org/papers/w4148)
- Paul David (AER 1990), "The Dynamo and the Computer": electric motors took roughly four decades (1880s–1920s) to show up in manufacturing productivity, because gains required redesigning factories around unit drive, not merely swapping steam engines for dynamos. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2006600)
- Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson, "The Productivity J-Curve" (NBER WP 25148, 2018; AEJ Macro 2021): measured productivity initially *under*states a GPT's value because firms invest in unmeasured intangible complements (process redesign, training, data); the payoff appears later and is captured by those who built the complements. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [NBER WP 25148](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25148)
- Teece (1986), "Profiting from Technological Innovation": when imitation is easy, profits flow to owners of *complementary assets* (distribution, manufacturing, data, reputation), not to the innovator or early user. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [Research Policy](https://doi.org/10.1016/0048-7333(86)90027-2)
- Bick, Blandin & Deming, "The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI" (NBER WP 32966, 2024; revised through Oct 2025): 39% of US adults 18–64 had used generative AI by Aug 2024; 24% of workers used it in the prior week, ~1 in 9 every workday; adoption faster than the PC or internet at comparable stages. [verified Oct 2026] — [NBER WP 32966](https://nber.org/papers/w32966); [St. Louis Fed version](https://fedinprint.org/item/fedlwp/98805/original)
- Narayanan & Kapoor (2025): despite ~40% adult usage, generative AI accounts for only 0.5–3.5% of US work hours — adoption of the *tool* is fast, integration into *work* is slow. [verified Oct 2026] — [slides](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/7bcefa22eb96683201bf2d93253cf71c-0050022025/original/Paper-1-Narayanan-ABCDE-AI-Normal-Technology.pdf)
- Prompt engineering as a case study of erosion: Indeed searches for "prompt engineer" roles peaked in April 2023; by May 2025 Fortune reported the "$200K role" was "obsolete," Indeed economist Allison Shrivastava said the skill is "still definitely a good thing to have, but it's not an entire title," and the WSJ (via TechRepublic) called the title "basically obsolete" as companies instead train all employees to use AI. Generative-AI terms appeared in only ~3 of 1,000 Indeed postings. [verified Oct 2026] — [Fortune, May 7, 2025](https://www.fortune.com/2025/05/07/prompt-engineering-200k-six-figure-role-now-obsolete-thanks-to-ai); [TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-prompt-engineering-ai-jobs-obsolete/)
- AI-skill wage premia (job-ad based, measure *specialist* AI skills): PwC 2026 Barometer: 62% premium for roles requiring AI skills, up from 57% in 2025 (another source says 56%), ranging from 118% (consumer markets) to 16% (government); postings requiring AI skills grew 69% vs 9% overall. Lightcast (US): postings requiring AI skills pay ~28% more (~$18,000/yr); only ~2.5–2.8% of US postings required AI skills in 2025. These do not control for education, experience or location and are not evidence of returns to an individual learning the skill. [verified Oct 2026] — [PwC press release](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html); [Robert Walters summary of Lightcast](https://www.robertwalters.us/insights/hiring-advice/blog/ai-salary-premium.html); [AI Job Risk Check summary](https://aijobriskcheck.com/do-ai-skills-pay-more)
- Alekseeva, Azar, Giné, Samila & Taska (Labour Economics, 2021): pre-generative-AI vacancy data showed an ~11% within-firm wage premium for AI skills, concentrated in technical roles. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [Labour Economics](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.labeco.2021.102002)
- Felten, Raj & Seamans (2019): occupations more exposed to (pre-generative) AI saw small positive wage changes and no employment change on average. [verified Oct 2026] — [SSRN 3368605](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3368605)
- Apollo (2026): real wage growth 6.7% slower in jobs where AI is used — if confirmed, suggests the *user-level* premium is not materializing even as the *specialist* premium rises. [verified Oct 2026, secondary] — [The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/apollo-ai-wage-growth-not-job-losses-study)
- Historical analog, spreadsheets: NPR Planet Money (2015) documents that after VisiCalc/Lotus (1979–83) the US lost ~400,000 bookkeeping/accounting-clerk jobs while gaining ~600,000 accountant/analyst jobs, as the tool became universal within roughly a decade and the advantage shifted to those who could ask better questions of the model. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [Planet Money episode 606](https://www.npr.org/sections/money/2015/02/25/389027988/episode-606-spreadsheets)
- Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) on the moving frontier: the "jagged frontier" of what AI can do is not fixed and expands with each model release, so knowledge of *where* the frontier lies is itself a depreciating asset. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [SSRN 4573321](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321)
- Anthropic "Cadences": heavier delegators report learning at the same rate as others, but the authors say skill erosion "cannot be ruled out." [verified Oct 2026] — [Cadences](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report)

### Inferences
- The professor's claim (3) — "large now, diminishing over time" — is directionally consistent with the diffusion literature for the *tool-use* component of the advantage, and the prompt-engineering episode suggests the erosion half-life for a pure tool skill is on the order of 1–2 years, not a decade. However, the GPT literature implies the opposite shape for the *complement* component: advantages built on redesigned workflows, proprietary data and domain judgment grow *over* time (J-curve), which is why Teece-style complementary assets, not early adoption, determine who profits.
- Rising specialist AI-skill premia (PwC 62%) alongside flat-to-negative wage effects for ordinary users (Apollo, Humlum) is exactly the pattern one would expect if generalist facility with AI is commoditizing while scarce complements (ML engineering, domain-plus-AI integration) are not.
- Diminishing returns is the wrong model for the complement: the right model is a race between how fast the tool democratizes (fast) and how fast the user converts it into non-imitable assets (slow, path-dependent).

### Gaps
- No study directly measures the half-life of a generative-AI *usage* skill premium; the PwC/Lightcast series measure specialist roles and cannot speak to the professor's "skilled human supervisor" advantage.
- No research was found on the diffusion of statistical software or spreadsheets specifically in academia and the duration of early-adopter advantage there.
- Alekseeva et al.'s premium trend through 2026 and the Lightcast "Beyond the Buzz" primary report could not be opened.

## Key Question 5: What do economists and strategy scholars recommend for individuals positioning during the transition, and which human roles do they predict remain scarce?

### Takeaway
Across otherwise disagreeing camps, the recommended individual strategy converges on *complementarity*: position in tasks where cheap AI prediction/drafting raises the value of human judgment, accountability, and relationships (Agrawal–Gans–Goldfarb; Autor; Mollick), while avoiding competing with AI on codified execution; Korinek adds scenario planning and diversification because the AGI tail is unresolved; Acemoglu stresses that whether AI complements or substitutes is a *choice* firms and policy make, not a technological given. Roles economists most often predict remain scarce: judgment under uncertainty, accountability/legitimacy for decisions, relationships and trust, context-specific tacit knowledge, and physical/in-person work; ownership of data and distribution is emphasized more by strategy scholars (Teece) than by labor economists.

### Cited Findings
- Agrawal, Gans & Goldfarb, "The Simple Economics of Machine Intelligence" (HBR, Nov 2016) and *Prediction Machines* (2018) / *Power and Prediction* (2022): AI lowers the cost of *prediction*; the value of complements — *judgment* (knowing which outcomes matter), data, and action — rises; humans should move toward judgment and system redesign. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [HBR 2016](https://hbr.org/2016/11/the-simple-economics-of-machine-intelligence)
- David Autor: AI should be used to extend "expertise" — the ability to make higher-stakes decisions — to more workers with foundational training, rebuilding the middle class rather than hollowing it; "new work" arises from augmentation, and 60% of today's jobs did not exist in 1940. [verified Oct 2026] — [Noema essay PDF](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-04/NOEMA-Autor-Feb-2024.pdf); [NBER "Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs"](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140)
- Acemoglu, Autor & Johnson, "Can We Have Pro-Worker AI?" (MIT Shaping the Future of Work, 2023) and Acemoglu & Johnson, *Power and Progress* (2023): the direction of AI (automation vs augmentation) is chosen; policy and firm incentives currently tilt toward automation; recommend steering toward tools that create new tasks for workers. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [MIT Shaping the Future of Work](https://shapingwork.mit.edu/research/can-we-have-pro-worker-ai-choosing-a-path-of-machines-in-service-of-minds/)
- Korinek, "Scenario Planning for an AGI Future" (IMF F&D, Dec 2023): economists and individuals should plan across scenarios (business-as-usual, AGI in 20 years, AGI in 5 years) rather than bet on one; the 2026 Korinek et al. model shows outcomes for knowledge-worker wages range from flat (Substantial) to −10%+ (Extreme). [IMF prior knowledge, not re-fetched; 2026 verified] — [IMF F&D](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/2023/12/Scenario-Planning-for-an-AGI-future-Anton-korinek); [Anthropic Institute scenarios](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios)
- Mollick (in Dell'Acqua et al. 2023 and *Co-Intelligence*, 2024): "centaur" (clear division of labor) and "cyborg" (continuous integration) work styles; the practical recommendation is to learn the jagged frontier of one's own domain through use. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [SSRN 4573321](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=4573321)
- Narayanan & Kapoor (2025): as AI diffuses, human roles shift toward specification, oversight and control of AI systems; they argue the "capability layer" is over-weighted and that reliability and diffusion-relevant properties should be measured instead. [verified Oct 2026] — [Knight Institute essay](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology); [Stanford talk listing](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/event/arvind-narayanan-adapting-to-the-transformation-of-knowledge-work/)
- Tyler Cowen: the winners in the AI era are those with initiative and the ability to deploy AI in sectors slow to adopt; "initiative beats intelligence." [verified Oct 2026, secondary; full text not opened] — [Hvylya analysis](https://en.hvylya.net/analytics/2659-initiative-beats-intelligence-tyler-cowen-on-the-winners-and-losers-of-the-ai-era)
- Dwarkesh Patel (2025): the binding constraint on replacing white-collar labor is on-the-job (continual) learning; until solved, humans who accumulate firm-specific context remain the integrators. [verified Oct 2026] — [Zvi summary](https://thezvi.substack.com/p/dwarkesh-patel-on-continual-learning)
- Empirical anchors for "what stays scarce": Anthropic's survey of experienced workers names judgment, context and relationship-building; PwC's "professionalised" roles (AI supporting expert judgment) grow fastest; Microsoft's applicability scores are lowest for physical occupations; Cowen's slow-adopting sectors (government, healthcare, education, nonprofits) are ~half of GDP. [verified Oct 2026] — [Cadences](https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report); [PwC](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html); [Cowen/Dwarkesh](https://dwarkesh.com/p/tyler-cowen-4)

- Teece (1986) for the strategy-scholar view: profits from a widely imitable technology accrue to holders of complementary assets — in the AI context, proprietary data, distribution, brand/reputation, and institutional access. [prior knowledge, not re-fetched] — [Research Policy](https://doi.org/10.1016/0048-7333(86)90027-2)

### Inferences
- The literature's advice is not "become a skilled supervisor of AI" as a standalone skill; it is "own a complement." Supervision is valuable only when bundled with the domain judgment that makes supervision possible (Q3 evidence) and with accountability that cannot be delegated (a signature, a license, a reputation).
- For an academic strategist specifically, the complements the literature points to are: field-specific tacit knowledge of what counts as a good question, access to proprietary or hard-to-collect data, institutional legitimacy (peer review, credentials, teaching accreditation), and relationships (co-authors, practitioners) — not facility with the tools, which the diffusion evidence says will be universal within a few years.

### Gaps
- No systematic survey of *strategy* scholars (as opposed to economists) on individual positioning was found.
- Economists' lists of "scarce human roles" are largely argued, not measured; only the Anthropic survey and PwC job-ad split offer data, and both are vendor-produced.

## Key Question 6: Macro scenario families, their proponents and evidence, and the base rates of technology-timeline predictions

### Takeaway
Three scenario families define the debate. (A) Gradual complementarity (Autor, Acemoglu, Cowen, Narayanan–Kapoor, Yale Budget Lab, Goldman Sachs; Korinek's "Modest/Substantial") is supported by all realized aggregate data through Aug 2026. (B) Mass white-collar displacement within a decade (Amodei; Goldman's 9% displacement figure, though with reemployment; Korinek's "Substantial" for knowledge workers) is supported by the entry-level canaries and task-market losses but not by unemployment data. (C) Intelligence-explosion/transformative AI (AI 2027; Korinek's "Extreme") rests on capability trends (METR time horizons doubling every ~4–7 months; compute 5x/year) whose own authors graded reality at 58–66% of their predicted pace by Feb 2026. Base rates — Armstrong & Sotala's finding that AI predictions cluster 15–25 years out regardless of when they are made, and the 40-year electrification lag — counsel discounting confident near-term timelines while not ruling out the tail.

### Cited Findings

**Scenario A — gradual complementarity [EXPERT OPINION / EMPIRICAL]**

The Aug 2026 economist survey's typical respondent sat near the Substantial scenario (~5% unemployment). [verified Oct 2026] — [Anthropic Institute](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/econ-scenarios)

### Inferences
- Realized 2023–26 data are consistent with Scenario A on aggregates and with the *early stages* of Scenario B at the entry-level margin; nothing in labor-market data yet distinguishes B from A because both predict small aggregate effects in the first 3–4 years. The discriminating evidence in 2026–27 will be whether the entry-level gap spreads to prime-age incumbents and whether METR-style horizons keep doubling past the suite's 16-hour reliability ceiling.
- Scenario C's own proponents have revised toward slower timelines (AI 2027 at 58–66% pace; Dwarkesh 2032; Grace et al. 2047 median), so the professor's confident "replaces the knowledge economy" should be read as a possible tail (perhaps the ~25% Manifold puts on >10% unemployment by 2030), not a base case.
- For individual planning, the asymmetry matters: Scenario A rewards building complements (Q4–Q5); Scenario C makes most individual positioning moot; Scenario B rewards exactly the judgment/accountability/relationship complements plus financial and institutional diversification. The Korinek-style recommendation — plan across scenarios — dominates picking one.
