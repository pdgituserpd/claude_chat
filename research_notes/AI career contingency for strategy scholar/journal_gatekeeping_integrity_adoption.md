## Key Question 1: Stated AI policies (authorship, disclosure, analysis/writing, detection) at top strategy/management journals and their publishers, and 2025–2026 editorials on AI-generated manuscripts

### Takeaway
As of October 2026 every relevant publisher (Wiley, SAGE, INFORMS, Elsevier, Cambridge) and the Academy of Management requires disclosure of substantive generative-AI use, bars AI authorship, and places full accountability on human authors; none bans AI in analysis or writing, and only Wiley explicitly forbids AI from creating or altering research data/results. The decisive 2026 development is the Organization Science editorial "More Versus Better" (Gartenberg, Hasan, Murray & Pierce, online 27 Apr 2026), which measured AI content in the journal's own submission corpus and frames AI-driven volume growth as an "emerging crisis in peer review."

### Cited Findings

**Academy of Management (AMJ, AMR, AMD, AMLE, AMP, Annals)**
- AOM has "adopted an AI Policy at the point of submission for journals and the Annual Meeting," stated to be "consistent with the Committee on Publication Ethics (C.O.P.E) position statement on AI"; core rule: "Work submitted to AOM must be created by the authors and not the product of artificial intelligence tools unless appropriate to the research question and properly cited"; guiding principle that AI "must never replace human judgment or accountability"; the policy "may evolve further" — [AOM AI Policy](https://www.aom.org/publications/journals/publishing-with-aom/aom-artificial-intelligence-policy/); [AOM publishing policies and ethics](https://www.aom.org/publications/publishing-policies-and-ethics/)
- Per a search summary of the AOM policy page, journal authors "must disclose AI use in the cover letter and the article acknowledgements, effective immediately" (cover-letter + acknowledgements placement could not be verified against the live page) — [AOM AI Policy](https://aom.org/research/publishing-with-aom/aom-artificial-intelligence-policy)
- No AMJ- or AMR-specific editorial on AI-generated manuscripts was surfaced; the AOM-wide policy is the operative rule for those journals (searched Oct 2026) — [search context](https://car.aom.org/discussion/jms-launches-new-policy-on-the-use-of-ai-in-research)
- AOM 2026 Annual Meeting (Philadelphia) panel symposium "The Stakeholder Dilemma: Disclosure, Trust, and Accountability for AI in Research" (ID 13902) asks "when does AI disclosure build trust, and when does it become a stigma cue?" and plans to produce an "AI Disclosure Framework, a Verification Checklist, and an Accountability Allocation Map" — [OMT Division listing](https://omt.aom.org/discussion/aom-2026-panel-symposium-the-stakeholder-dilemma-disclosure-trust-and-accountability-for-ai-in-research-id-13902-5); [HR Division listing](https://hr.aom.org/discussion/aom-2026-panel-symposium-the-stakeholder-dilemma-disclosure-trust-and-accountability-for-ai-in-research-id-13902-3?hlmlt=VT)

**Wiley (publisher of SMJ, GSJ, SEJ, JMS)**
- Wiley "welcomes the thoughtful use of AI tools"; "Authors may only use AI Technology as a companion to their writing process, not a replacement"; "If an author has used AI Technology to develop any portion of a manuscript, its use must be described, transparently and in detail, in the Methods section" (or disclosure/Acknowledgements where applicable); authors "should maintain documentation of all AI Technology used, including its purpose"; disclosure required "upon submission" and may be requested "during the submission and peer review process, or after publication" — [Wiley AI Guidelines (PDF copy, Apr 2025)](https://the-hospitalist.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Wiley-AI-Guidelines.pdf); [Research Information](https://www.researchinformation.info/news/wiley-releases-ai-guidelines-for-authors/)
- Wiley is the strictest on data: "AI Technology must not be used to create, alter, or manipulate original research data or results including data visualization, illustrations and photographs"; AI translation permitted if disclosed; grammar/spell-check reportedly exempt from disclosure (secondary mirror) — [Enago mirror of Wiley guidelines](https://www.enago.com/responsible-ai-movement/publisher-ai-guidelines/wiley-ai-guidelines/)
- Journal of Management Studies (Wiley) editorial, Gatrell, Muzio, Post & Wickert, "Here, There and Everywhere: On the Responsible Use of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in Management Research and the Peer-Review Process," JMS 61(3): 739–751 (2024): allows "supervised use of AI in research" in both "authoring of texts and in data analyses" provided it is "transparently explained, disclosed and justified to readers"; author teams must "oversee all aspects of AI use within their projects, and ... take personal responsibility for accuracy"; prohibits "AI's use in peer-reviewers' evaluations of submissions, and regarding editors' handling of manuscripts" — [JMS editorial (author PDF)](https://business-society.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/J-Management-Studies-2024-Gatrell-Here-there-and-Everywhere-On-the-Responsible-Use-of-Artificial-Intelligence-AI-.pdf); [JMS policy announcement](https://car.aom.org/discussion/jms-launches-new-policy-on-the-use-of-ai-in-research); [Liverpool repository](https://livrepository.liverpool.ac.uk/3178887/)
- Strategic Management Journal, Global Strategy Journal and Strategic Entrepreneurship Journal: no journal-specific AI policy text or AI editorial was surfaced by any search; the June 2023 SMJ Author Instructions PDF predates most AI policy adoption — [SMJ Author Instructions, 8 Jun 2023](https://www.strategicmanagement.net/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SMJ-Author-Instructions-06-08-23-FINAL.pdf)

**SAGE (publisher of ASQ, Journal of Management, Strategic Organization)**
- SAGE "requires authors to disclose at submission when AI tools and/or LLMs are used primarily or partially to generate manuscript content" affecting methods, analysis, results or conclusions; pure language polishing exempt ("Authors are not required to disclose the use of these assistive AI tools"); disclosure should state "which model was used and for what purpose" in methods or acknowledgements; "AI bots such as ChatGPT should not be listed as an author"; authors must check for fabricated facts and "citations that don't exist"; "if the Editor becomes aware that Generative AI was inappropriately used in the preparation of a submission without disclosure, the Editor reserves the right to reject the submission"; "Editors must not use ChatGPT or other Generative AI to generate decision letters, or summaries of unpublished research" — [SAGE AI policy](https://www.sagepub.com/journals/editorial-policies/artificial-intelligence-policy); [SAGE author FAQ](https://journalssolutions.sagepub.com/support/solutions/articles/7000090479-what-is-sage-s-ai-policy-for-authors-); [SAGE editor FAQ](https://journalssolutions.sagepub.com/support/solutions/articles/7000090481-what-is-sage-s-ai-policy-for-editors-)
- ASQ's SAGE-hosted submission document contains the fragment: "If you use AI-powered tools during manuscript preparation, as the submitting author you are fully responsible for the accuracy and presentation of your [manuscript]" (full ASQ text not retrievable) — [ASQ submission guidelines (SAGE)](https://www.sagepub.com/docs/default-source/msg/ASQ)

**INFORMS (Management Science, Organization Science)**
- Management Science submission guidelines include an "Artificial Intelligence Policy" that does not ban generative AI in manuscript preparation; it places responsibility on authors for accuracy, treating submission as a declaration that they reviewed and edited any AI-generated content; reviewers are directed to notify the Associate Editor and/or Department Editor "if a reviewer sees strong evidence that the use of an AI tool in a paper caused errors" — [Management Science Submission Guidelines](https://pubsonline.informs.org/page/mnsc/submission-guidelines)
- INFORMS maintains a cross-journal "Guidelines for Reviewers and Editors, including the use of Generative AI" (linked from the Marketing Science page; text not retrieved); no Organization Science-specific author AI policy page was surfaced — [Marketing Science calls page](https://pubsonline.informs.org/page/mksc/calls-for-papers)

**Elsevier (Research Policy)**
- Elsevier requires a section titled "Declaration of generative AI and AI-assisted technologies in the manuscript preparation process" placed "at the end of the manuscript, immediately before the reference list"; an updated journal policy (2025–26, exact date not retrieved) gives "authors, reviewers and editors clearer guidance on how AI tools may be used, what must be disclosed"; "Authors remain fully responsible for everything they submit. That includes checking AI-generated material for accuracy, bias, completeness and fabricated or incorrect references" — [Elsevier updated GenAI policy (FR page)](https://www.elsevier.com/fr-fr/connect/updated-generative-ai-policies-for-journals-supporting-responsible-use-while-protecting-trust); [CASRAI summary](https://casrai.org/guides/elsevier-generative-ai-authorship-policy)

**Cambridge University Press (Management and Organization Review, Journal of Management & Organization)**
- CUP's AI research-ethics policy (13 Mar 2023): "AI and LLM tools may not be listed as an author on any scholarly work published by Cambridge"; AI use "must be declared and clearly explained in publications such as research papers, just as we expect scholars to do with other software, tools and methodologies"; authors accountable for accuracy and originality — [Cambridge news release](https://www.cambridge.org/sv/news-and-insights/cambridge-launches-ai-research-ethics-policy); [infoDOCKET, 14 Mar 2023](https://www.infodocket.com/2023/03/14/cambridge-university-press-releases-first-ai-research-ethics-policy/); [CUP AI Policy for Content Creators (PDF)](https://www.cambridge.org/authorhub/application/files/7617/2743/0832/AI_Policy_for_Content_Creators.pdf)

**Cross-publisher pattern and enforcement evidence**
- 2023 bibliometric analysis of publisher/journal instructions: among those with guidance, "94.1% of publishers and 95.7% of journals prohibited the inclusion of GAI as an author" — [arXiv 2307.11918](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11918v1)
- Preprint (Dec 2025) analysing 5,114 journals: "70% of journals adopting AI policies (primarily requiring disclosure)"; AI-writing-tool use "has risen regardless of whether a journal had a policy"; "the actual effectiveness of these policies remains unclear" — [arXiv 2512.06705, "Academic journals' AI policies fail to curb the surge in AI-assisted academic writing"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06705)
- Comparative publisher-policy landscape pages (secondary) note Springer and Wiley require disclosure in Methods or Acknowledgements — [CASRAI publisher landscape](https://www.casrai.org/guides/ai-in-manuscripts-publisher-policy-landscape); [Thesify 2025 overview](https://www.thesify.ai/blog/ai-policies-academic-publishing-2025)

**2025–2026 management editorials on AI-generated manuscripts / submission floods**
- Gartenberg, Hasan, Murray & Pierce (Pierce = Organization Science Editor-in-Chief), "More Versus Better: Artificial Intelligence, Incentives, and the Emerging Crisis in Peer Review," Organization Science 37(3), published online 27 Apr 2026, DOI 10.1287/orsc.2026.ed.v37.n3 — [DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2026.ed.v37.n3); [Wharton PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/More-Versus-Better.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/404219750_More_Versus_Better_Artificial_Intelligence_Incentives_and_the_Emerging_Crisis_in_Peer_Review); [Org Science Substack "More Versus Better, Part I"](https://orgsci.substack.com/p/more-versus-better-part-i); [WashU Source, May 2026](https://source.washu.edu/2026/05/analysis-reveals-ais-impact-on-research-journals/)
- The editorial's data: "6,957 submissions by 11,887 authors, reviewed 10,389 times by 2,519 unique reviewers," Jan 2021–Feb 2026; AI content measured with the Pangram detector, "trained to minimize false positives"; authors say they "might, if anything, understate the prevalence of AI writing in 2026 submissions and reviews" — [WashU Source](https://source.washu.edu/2026/05/analysis-reveals-ais-impact-on-research-journals/); [Yahoo/press summary](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/ai-slop-flooding-academic-journals-210050774.html)

- Boston Globe (2 Aug 2026): medical journals, law reviews and literary magazines "grappling with AI-generated submissions" (details not retrieved) — [Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/08/02/business/literary-academic-journals-artificial-intelligence/)

### Inferences
- The policy regime is disclosure-based, not prohibition-based: an AI-heavy empirical pipeline is permissible at every top management outlet if disclosed, but Wiley's data clause means any AI step that "creates or alters" results (e.g., automated variable construction or imputation that is not fully human-verified) is a gray zone at SMJ/GSJ/SEJ/JMS specifically.
- Enforcement has shifted from honor-system disclosure to measurement: Organization Science has already run a detector over its full corpus (2021–2026) with IRB-style approval; other INFORMS/AOM/SMS editors now have a template for doing the same, so undisclosed heavy AI writing is increasingly detectable at the journal level even if not yet acted upon per-manuscript.
- The AOM 2026 "stigma cue" panel signals that disclosure itself may carry reputational cost in management reviewing in 2026, even though it is required — a double bind for high-volume AI users.

### Gaps
- No journal-specific AI policy text was retrievable for SMJ, GSJ, SEJ, Organization Science, Journal of Management, Strategic Organization, or Research Policy (publisher-level rules above are presumed to govern). The live AOM page could not be fetched to confirm the cover-letter/acknowledgements requirement.
- No 2025–2026 AI editorials from AMJ, AMR, ASQ, Management Science, Journal of Management, Strategic Organization or Research Policy were surfaced; the Org Science editorial is the only management-journal AI-flood editorial found.
- Whether any top management journal uses AI detectors routinely at desk-review stage (vs. the one-off Org Science study) is unknown.

## Key Question 2: Capacity constraints (submission volumes, desk-rejection and acceptance rates, time-to-decision, reviewer shortages) and documented AI-driven submission growth

### Takeaway
Top management journals accept roughly 7–10% of submissions and were already strained before generative AI; the only management-specific causal evidence of AI-driven growth is Organization Science's 42% submission jump attributed "almost entirely" to AI-heavy manuscripts, consistent with platform-wide (+33% YoY, Q1 2026) and adjacent-field (public administration, development economics) data. Editors' responses so far are submission caps (JPART, July 2026), rising desk rejection, and measurement/detection; no management journal fee or cap was found.

**Management journal baselines**

**Modelled consequences**
- "Tenure Under Pressure: Simulating the Disruptive Effects of AI on Academic Publishing" (Sept 2025) simulates AI-driven submission growth against fixed reviewer capacity using AMJ (~8–10%) and SMJ (~7%) acceptance baselines (results not retrieved) — [arXiv 2509.16925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.16925)
- Anthropic's Feb–Mar 2026 survey of 1,260 quantitative social scientists: coding-agent users report ~75% more working papers posted and ~10% more empirical projects started than comparable non-users, but "no evidence of a difference" in journal submissions/resubmissions; respondents "worried about more papers creating congestion and competition for attention" and that AI "could worsen selective reporting and push research toward risk-averse, incremental work" — [Anthropic, "Coding agents and the social sciences"](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)

### Inferences
- A 7–10% acceptance rate with a 42% volume surge concentrated in low-readability AI-heavy manuscripts implies that the marginal AI-assisted paper at a top management journal is being rejected at above-average rates; volume alone is a negative signal unless the writing is indistinguishable from careful human prose.
- Because the Org Science authors found higher rejection rates for AI-heavy manuscripts, the realistic expected yield of a "many papers quickly" strategy at A-journals is lower than the historical base rate, not higher.
- Management has not yet adopted caps or fees (none found), but the JPART precedent and the Org Science editorial's "crisis" framing suggest these tools are now on the table for INFORMS/AOM/SMS journals in 2026–27.

### Gaps
- No official 2024–2026 submission counts, desk-rejection rates or decision times for SMJ, AMJ, AMR, ASQ, JOM, Strategic Organization, Research Policy, GSJ or SEJ were retrievable.
- Rejection rates by AI-content tier inside the Org Science study were not retrieved numerically.
- No evidence found of any management journal adopting submission fees, caps, or routine AI detection at desk; the AER/JPE editors' reports were not surfaced.

## Key Question 3: Research-integrity norms bearing on a "generate many questions → construct variables → run models → keep significant results" pipeline (publication bias, HARKing, specification search, pre-registration, replication, open science)

### Takeaway
Strategic management has a documented, journal-endorsed literature (Bettis 2012; Goldfarb & King 2016; Bergh et al. 2017; Bettis et al. 2016 SMJ editorials) identifying specification search and the "search for asterisks" as the field's central credibility problem, estimating that 24–40% of published significant coefficients in SMJ/AMJ/OrgSci/ASQ/MS would not replicate. A pipeline that explicitly selects on significance is the textbook case these authors condemn; if disclosed it would be read as HARKing/p-hacking at scale, yet top management journals still mostly lack the registered-report and pre-registration infrastructure (only MOR, LQ and a JBP-led consortium offer it) that would let such a pipeline be run legitimately.

### Cited Findings

**Strategic management's own diagnosis**
- Bettis, R.A. (2012), "The search for asterisks: Compromised statistical tests and flawed theories," Strategic Management Journal 33(1): 108–113 — [IDEAS/RePEc listing](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v37y2016i11p2193-2203.html); [Wikipedia biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_A._Bettis)
- Goldfarb & King (2016), "Scientific apophenia in strategic management research: Significance tests & mistaken inference," SMJ 37(1): 167–176: based on 300 papers (six per journal per year, 2003–2012, from SMJ, AMJ, Organization Science, ASQ, Management Science) they estimate that "if each study were repeated, 24–40 percent of significant coefficients would become insignificant at the five percent level"; for about half of these "the true coefficient is very close to 0," the rest "directionally correct but inflated in magnitude"; they define the problem as "our tendency to find and publish evidence of order where none exists," cite Bettis (2012) that specification searches "can greatly increase the probability of finding an apparently meaningful relationship in random data," and call it "a serious problem in the literature on strategic management" — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v37y2016i1p167-176.html); [FORRT summary](https://forrt.org/curated_resources/scientific-apophenia-in-strategic-manage/); [UMD Smith, 13 Jan 2015, "Are one-third of strategic management studies bogus?"](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/news/are-one-third-strategic-management-studies-bogus); [Tuck, "How bias creeps into scientific research"](https://tuck.dartmouth.edu/news/articles/how-bias-creeps-into-scientific-research)

- A joint multi-journal trial of a hybrid registered-reports option includes Journal of Business and Psychology, The Leadership Quarterly and several other management/I-O journals; papers judged "on rigor rather than results" (page undated) — [JBP site](https://jbp.charlotte.edu/?p=100); The Leadership Quarterly was among 277 journals offering registered reports in a 2021 Center for Open Science count — [HRMJ editorial via Aston](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/document/80274)
- Briker & Gerpott (Organizational Research Methods, 2024) reviewed 50 registered reports in management and applied psychology and surveyed authors, reviewers and editors to derive best practices — [Maastricht CRIS](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/publishing-registered-reports-in-management-and-applied-psycholog/); "How to Conduct and Publish Open Science in Management Research" — [BFH ARBOR](https://arbor.bfh.ch/20946)
- O'Boyle (lead author), "The Chrysalis Effect: How Ugly Initial Results Metamorphosize Into Beautiful Articles," Journal of Management — compares dissertations with their published versions as evidence of questionable research practices (exact ratios not retrieved) — [Social Science Space podcast, Aug 2014](https://www.socialsciencespace.com/2014/08/listen-to-the-latest-podcast-from-journal-of-management-on-the-chrysalis-effect/)

**Adjacent evidence: economics and finance on p-hacking at scale (labelled adjacent)**
- Brodeur, Cook & Heyes (2020, AER), "Methods Matter: P-Hacking and Causal Inference in Economics": 21,740 two-sided t-statistics from 25 leading economics journals (2015–2018); "p-hacking and publication bias vary by method, with IV (and to a lesser extent DID) ... particularly problematic" — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3249910); [AEA](https://aeaweb.org/content/file?id=12747)
- Kranz & Pütz (2022, AER comment): after adjusting for rounding, "statistical evidence for p-hacking ... at the 5 percent significance threshold vanishes for difference-in-difference studies but remains for instrumental variable studies" — [AER](https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20210121)
- "When is p-hacking detectable?" (arXiv, Jun 2025) re-analyses the Brodeur corpus: Elliott et al. (2022) tests "generally found no evidence of p-hacking in any of these subsamples," while the new projection test finds "selective reporting for RCTs, IVs, and DIDs" — [arXiv 2506.20035](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.20035); Elliott et al. "Detecting p-hacking" — [arXiv 1906.06711](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.06711)
- Brodeur, Carrell, Figlio & Lusher (2023) track p-hacking across the publication process with Journal of Human Resources data; reproduced by the Institute for Replication (2025) — [I4R reproduction report](https://www.rwi-essen.de/i4r-discussion-paper-series/1/reproduction-report-on-unpacking-p-hacking-and-260804006); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:zbw:i4rdps:305)
- Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016, Review of Financial Studies 29(1): 5–68), "... and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns": "Hundreds of papers and hundreds of factors attempt to explain the cross-section of expected returns. Given this extensive data mining, it does not make any economic or statistical sense to use the usual significance criteria for a newly discovered factor, e.g., a t-ratio greater than 2.0"; "a newly discovered factor needs to clear a much higher hurdle, with a t-ratio greater than 3.0"; "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false" — [NBER w20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592); [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2513152)
- Counterpoint: "Most claimed statistical findings in cross-sectional return predictability are likely true" (arXiv, 2022) — [arXiv 2206.15365](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.15365)
- Z-curve 3.0 tutorial (Sept 2025): to test for p-hacking, z-curve is fitted to z-values > 2.8 (p = .005); "non-significant results do not imply that there is no bias" — [Replication Index](https://replicationindex.com/2025/09/02/z-curve-3-0-tutorial-chapter-9)

**AI-specific integrity signals**
- Anthropic 2026 survey respondents "feared AI could worsen selective reporting and push research toward risk-averse, incremental work" — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)
- Management Science requires reviewers to flag "strong evidence that the use of an AI tool in a paper caused errors" to the AE/DE — [Management Science Submission Guidelines](https://pubsonline.informs.org/page/mnsc/submission-guidelines)
- A 2025 "10x surge" in AI-related retractions is reported: ">200 papers retracted or investigated in 2025 over undisclosed or inappropriate AI use," including a single Neurosurgical Review wave of 129 papers (fields not management-specific) — [Enago](https://www.enago.com/responsible-ai-movement/resources/10x-surge-ai-retractions-llm-career-risk-research)

### Inferences
- "Keep the significant, drop the rest" is operationally identical to the specification search Bettis (2012) and Goldfarb & King (2016) describe; because SMJ co-editors published these critiques as editorials, a disclosed description of such a pipeline would almost certainly be rejected at SMJ, and would be treated as a questionable research practice at AMJ/JOM, whose own methods literature (Aguinis, Banks, O'Boyle) defines HARKing and outcome-reporting bias as QRPs.
- The realistic detection risk is low per-paper (no management journal pre-screens for specification search) but rising at the portfolio level: a scholar producing many AI-written papers in a short window now matches the exact profile Org Science flagged (AI-heavy text, lower readability, higher rejection), and reviewer/editor suspicion of "AI slop" transfers to the author's name across journals.
- The legitimate version of the same pipeline—pre-registered multiverse/specification-curve analysis reporting all models, or a registered report—faces an infrastructure gap in management (few RR outlets), which pushes such work to MOR, LQ, JBP, or to disclosing full specification sets in supplements at SMJ (which accepts null results in principle).
- Finance's factor-zoo response (t > 3.0 hurdle) is the clearest adjacent precedent for how a field re-prices evidence once cheap search is widespread; management reviewers trained on Goldfarb & King are primed to apply similar discounting.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve: SMJ's current (2024–2026) policy text on p-values/asterisks, effect sizes and reporting all specifications; whether SMJ/AMJ now offer registered reports; AMD or JOMSR registered-report details; Banks et al. QRP survey percentages; Bosco/Aguinis HARKing prevalence numbers; O'Boyle et al. Chrysalis-effect ratios; Brodeur, Cook, Hartley & Heyes (2024) pre-registration results; Jensen, Kelly & Pedersen (2023) replication findings; Harvey's 2017 presidential address text.
- No documented case was found of a management scholar penalized specifically for disclosed or detected specification search; the "actual risk of reputational harm" remains inferential.

## Key Question 4: Is the "contribution" bar changing because empirical execution is becoming cheap? Is AI-driven synthesis ("insight from 8,000 papers") valued or discounted?

### Takeaway
Direct editorial statements that top management journals are raising the theory or identification bar because of AI were not found; the strongest signal is the Organization Science editorial's framing that AI has produced "more" rather than "better" work and that AI-heavy manuscripts are "less original" and rejected more often, plus survey evidence that researchers themselves expect AI to push the field toward "risk-averse, incremental work." No evidence either way on how editors value large-scale AI synthesis.

### Cited Findings
- Organization Science EIC and co-authors conclude the AI-driven surge consists "mostly" of "more lower-quality, less original academic work rather than better research," and that heavier AI content correlates with higher rejection — [Yahoo/press summary of Gartenberg et al. 2026](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/ai-slop-flooding-academic-journals-210050774.html); [Org Science Substack](https://orgsci.substack.com/p/more-versus-better-part-i)
- AI-heavy manuscripts "require a higher grade level to parse, use more nominalizations and carry more jargon," i.e., the detectable signature is in prose quality, which reviewers read as a proxy for thinking quality — [Oregon Lundquist summary](https://business.uoregon.edu/news/ai-deluge-upending-academia)
- Social scientists surveyed in 2026 were markedly less optimistic about AI's effect on their field than on their own paper output: "88% rated AI's effect on publishable papers above 5 on a 1–10 scale," but "about 70% were more optimistic about paper productivity than about field impact," citing "congestion," "selective reporting," and "risk-averse, incremental work" — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)
- Wiley's 2025 survey finds researchers "significantly scaling back their expectations of what AI can currently do as they gain firsthand experience" — [Wiley newsroom, Oct 2025](https://newsroom.wiley.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2025/AI-Adoption-Jumps-to-84-Among-Researchers-as-Expectations-Undergo-Significant-Reality-Check/default.aspx)
- The AOM 2026 panel explicitly frames AI disclosure as a potential "stigma cue," implying reviewers may discount AI-assisted contributions — [OMT listing](https://omt.aom.org/discussion/aom-2026-panel-symposium-the-stakeholder-dilemma-disclosure-trust-and-accountability-for-ai-in-research-id-13902-5)
- Historical anchor: Goldfarb & King recommended replication "to insure that [studies] provide evidence for guiding managers," and SMJ's 2016 replication editorial sought "a cumulative body of knowledge" — the field's stated value is cumulative validity, not novelty volume — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/stratm/v37y2016i1p167-176.html); [SUFE summary](https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/378061)

### Inferences
- With empirical execution cheap and AI prose detectable, the scarce and rewarded inputs at A-journals in 2026 are (i) original framing/theory, (ii) proprietary or hard-to-assemble data, and (iii) credible identification; a pipeline optimizing for count on public data with generic hypotheses competes exactly where supply has exploded.
- Large-scale AI synthesis is more likely to be valued in review/annals outlets (Academy of Management Annals, JOM review issues) than as "contribution" in empirical A-journals, but this is an inference; no editor statement was found.

### Gaps
- No editorials from AMJ, AMR, SMJ, ASQ or Management Science on raising theory/identification standards in response to AI were surfaced (search budget exhausted before these queries ran).
- No evidence on how editors or committees treat AI-driven literature synthesis at scale.

## Key Question 5: Adoption evidence—how fast are management and adjacent researchers adopting AI tools, by task, and are business-school faculty laggards or leaders?

### Takeaway
Researcher AI use jumped from ~57% (2024) to 84% (2025) in Wiley's global survey and ~76% in OUP's 2024 survey, with substantive research-task use at 62% by Aug 2025; by early 2026, 81% of surveyed quantitative social scientists had tried AI and ~20% were regular coding-agent users (economists 39%), with code generation (77–97% of AI users) far ahead of prose drafting (~33%). Business-school faculty appear to be laggards relative to economists: AACSB's Feb 2025 survey found faculty use GenAI mostly for teaching, least for data analysis, and deans far more enthusiastic than faculty.

### Cited Findings

**Global researcher surveys**
- Wiley ExplanAItions 2025 (fielded Aug 2025, 2,430 researchers; released 7 Oct 2025): overall AI use rose "from 57% in 2024 to 84% in 2025"; use "for research and publication tasks ... grew significantly to 62% from 45%"; 85% say it improved efficiency; "close to three-quarters" say it enhanced quantity and quality; 57% cite "lack of guidelines and training as a primary barrier"; 73% look to publishers for guidelines; researchers "significantly scaling back their expectations" — [Wiley newsroom](https://newsroom.wiley.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2025/AI-Adoption-Jumps-to-84-Among-Researchers-as-Expectations-Undergo-Significant-Reality-Check/default.aspx); [Business Wire, 7 Oct 2025](https://businesswire.com/news/home/20251007928124/en/AI-Adoption-Jumps-to-84-Among-Researchers-as-Expectations-Undergo-Significant-Reality-Check); [Research Information](https://www.researchinformation.info/news/researchers-ai-expectations-undergo-significant-reality-check/)
- Wiley ExplanAItions 2024 (released Feb 2025; ~5,000 researchers, 70+ countries): expected AI use for manuscripts, grants and peer review to "become widely accepted within the next two years"; 63% cited lack of clear guidelines or need for training — [infoDOCKET, 4 Feb 2025](https://www.infodocket.com/2025/02/04/report-how-are-researchers-using-ai-survey-reveals-pros-and-cons-for-science/); [Proofig summary](https://www.proofig.com/news/researchers-want-more-guidance-from-publishers-on-how-to-document-ai)
- Oxford University Press (May 2024; 2,000+ / 2,300+ researchers): "76% of researchers use some form of AI in their research"; most common uses are finding, editing or summarizing existing research; machine translation and chatbots most popular; only 8% trust AI companies not to use their data without permission; 46% say their institution has no AI policy; 25% think AI will reduce need for critical thinking; 67% of users report benefit — [Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/news/use-ai-research-widespread-distrust-still-high-oup); [OUP blog, Jul 2024](https://blog.oup.com/2024/07/ai-attitudes-and-behaviour-researcher-profiles-interactive/); [UKSG release](https://uksg.org/?p=5333)
- Elsevier "Insights 2024: Attitudes toward AI" (fieldwork Dec 2023–Feb 2024; 2,999 respondents in 123 countries incl. 2,284 researchers): 94% of researchers expect AI to accelerate knowledge discovery; 95% expect it to be used for misinformation; 86% expect critical errors; "adoption of AI for work use remains low," with most non-users expecting to adopt "in the coming two to five years" — [Elsevier Insights page](https://www.elsevier.com/insights/attitudes-toward-ai); [Elsevier press release](https://www.elsevier.com/about/press-releases/the-insights-2024-attitudes-toward-ai-report-reveals-researchers-and-clinicians-believe-in-ai-potential-but-demand-transparency-in-order-to-trust-tools)
- Nature 2023 survey ("AI and science: what 1,600 researchers think"; ~1,659 researchers from >40,000 emailed) — [arXiv 2409.14570 using Nature data](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.14570); a separate Nature survey of 5,229 researchers (date unconfirmed, via aggregator) reported 28% use AI to edit papers and 8% for first drafts/summaries/translation/peer review — [CO/AI aggregator](https://getcoai.com/?p=68701)
- Coding-specific: a 2025 survey at one large institute found 43.2% used AI to "help write code"; in Nature's 2023 survey >40% agreed GenAI would make programming faster but actual use was not measured — [arXiv 2512.19644](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.19644); "How Researchers Use and Verify AI Coding Assistants" (2026) — [arXiv 2609.22049](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.22049)
- Sociology: survey of 433 authors in 50 sociology journals (Jan 2026) — [Sociological Science](https://sociologicalscience.com/?p=7935)

**Social-science / management-adjacent (Anthropic, fetched in full)**
- Survey of 1,260 quantitative social scientists, fielded late Feb–Mar 2026 (non-representative; recruited for a study offering Claude Max access); economics, political science and sociology ~20% each, "management sciences and psychology ... close behind"; ~40% full/associate professors, 25% assistant professors, 30% doctoral students — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)
- 81% have tried AI for research; 20% use coding agents more than weekly; among agent users Claude Code 86%, Codex 31%; regular agent use by discipline: economists 39%, political scientists 25%, public health 6%, communications 6%, education 4% (management not separately reported); doctoral students/postdocs just over 25% vs. tenured professors "less than half that rate"; male-named researchers adopt agents at more than twice the rate of female-named; top-university researchers ~40% more likely — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)
- Tasks: code generation 97% of agent users and 77% of other AI users; editing prose second; methods advice and background third; drafting prose only ~one-third of all AI users — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)
- Output: agent users report ~10% more empirical projects started, ~75% more working papers posted, more grant proposals, and no difference in journal submissions — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/coding-agents-social-sciences)

**Business-school specific**
- AACSB, "GenAI Adoption in Business Schools: Deans and Faculty Respond" (Feb 2025; 236 deans, 429 faculty): "GenAI remains a supplementary tool rather than a core component of most faculty members' workflows"; faculty "report implementing it more frequently in teaching than research"; "used less frequently for data analysis," where "security and reliability" worries are common; 46% of deans say their schools have AI/GenAI policies; "deans are expressing greater optimism ... while faculty remain more cautious" — [Poets&Quants, 3 Feb 2025](https://poetsandquants.com/2025/02/03/b-school-deans-love-ai-b-school-faculty-not-so-much-poll/); [DIGIT](https://www.digit.fyi/business-deans-embrace-genai-faculty-cautious/); [FE News](https://www.fenews.co.uk/?p=491107)
- Organization Science (management) corpus: by Feb 2026 a majority of submissions showed AI involvement, i.e., in manuscript text AI is already majority practice in management even if faculty self-report lags — [Yahoo/press summary](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/ai-slop-flooding-academic-journals-210050774.html)
- 5,114-journal study: AI-assisted writing rose "regardless of whether a journal had a policy" (2025) — [arXiv 2512.06705](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.06705)

### Inferences
- Writing/editing assistance is now near-universal and confers no edge; the current differentiator is agentic coding and end-to-end empirical pipelines, where regular use was ~20% among quantitative social scientists (39% in economics) in Mar 2026 and concentrated among junior, male, top-university researchers. If the 2024→2025 trajectory (57%→84% any use; 45%→62% research-task use) holds for agent use, regular agentic adoption plausibly exceeds half of active quantitative researchers by 2027–28, eroding a current adopter's speed advantage within roughly two to three years.
- Business-school faculty (per AACSB Feb 2025) lag economists in analytic AI use, so a management scholar using agents for empirics has a larger but shorter-lived relative edge than an economist would; the Org Science text data suggest that gap is closing fast in manuscript production.

### Gaps
- No AOM- or SMS-specific member survey of AI use was found; Ithaka S+R's May 2025 report ("Making AI Generative for Higher Education," DOI 10.18665/sr.322677) and Digital Science surveys were identified but their research-use percentages were not retrieved — [Ithaka S+R](https://sr.ithaka.org/?p=321161).
- Management is not separately broken out in the Anthropic survey; no time series of AI-for-data-analysis adoption specific to management exists in the sources found.
- Nature's 2025 survey figures could not be confirmed against the primary source.

## Key Question 6: Promotion-and-tenure committees, AACSB "intellectual contributions," and documented penalties/retractions for AI-assisted research in management or adjacent business disciplines

### Takeaway
No AACSB guidance tying generative AI to Standard 8 intellectual contributions, and no documented management/business-discipline retraction or P&T penalty specifically for AI-assisted research, were found; the available evidence is AACSB's Feb 2025 call for "more clear and actionable guidance," a Sept 2025 simulation of AI's effect on tenure, and cross-field 2025–26 retraction data (>200 AI-related retractions/investigations in 2025) that do not name business journals.

### Cited Findings
- AACSB's Feb 2025 report finds "more clear and actionable guidance is needed," including ethics training, and that 46% of deans report AI/GenAI policies in place; it addresses adoption, not how AI-assisted output counts toward intellectual contributions — [Poets&Quants, 3 Feb 2025](https://poetsandquants.com/2025/02/03/b-school-deans-love-ai-b-school-faculty-not-so-much-poll/); [DIGIT](https://www.digit.fyi/business-deans-embrace-genai-faculty-cautious/)
- "Tenure Under Pressure: Simulating the Disruptive Effects of AI on Academic Publishing" (Sept 2025) models AI-driven submission growth against tenure outcomes using AMJ/SMJ acceptance baselines (quantitative results not retrieved) — [arXiv 2509.16925](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.16925)
- Institutional example of research-AI guidance at a university level (The Citadel) — [Citadel guidelines](https://www.citadel.edu/ai-education/guidelines-for-the-use-of-generative-ai-in-research/)
- Cross-field retraction evidence: ">200 papers retracted or investigated in 2025 over undisclosed or inappropriate AI use," including 129 pulled by Neurosurgical Review; "inaccurate citations passing editorial review" (Nov 2025) — [Enago](https://www.enago.com/responsible-ai-movement/resources/10x-surge-ai-retractions-llm-career-risk-research)
- Retraction Watch's 2025 analysis treats GenAI-related breaches as a notable retraction category (MDPI blog summary, 23 Jul 2026) — [MDPI blog](https://blog.mdpi.com/2026/07/23/retractions-data-2025/)
- Hallucinated-citation case outside business: Disability Studies Quarterly paper flagged two weeks after March 2026 publication; editor's June 2026 editorial says "most hallucinated citations never get discovered or corrected" (Retraction Watch, 25 Sept 2026) — [Retraction Watch](https://retractionwatch.com/2026/09/25/above-and-beyond-an-editor-wanted-authors-to-know-theyd-been-falsely-cited/)
- A Springer Nature journal "clears AI papers" while one university's retractions "rise drastically" (2025) — [Retraction Watch 2025 archive](https://retractionwatch.com/2025/page/28)
- Study of 21 chatbots: they "identified fewer than half of retracted papers and produced many false positives" (15 May 2026) — [Rogue Scholar](https://rogue-scholar.org/records/kae0z-zza61)
- SAGE's policy makes undisclosed AI use a rejection ground, and Wiley's allows post-publication disclosure demands — the formal mechanisms by which a penalty would occur at ASQ/JOM/SO and SMJ/GSJ/SEJ — [SAGE AI policy](https://www.sagepub.com/journals/editorial-policies/artificial-intelligence-policy); [Wiley AI Guidelines PDF](https://the-hospitalist.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Wiley-AI-Guidelines.pdf)

### Inferences
- In the absence of AACSB or P&T rules, AI-assisted papers that clear peer review count as intellectual contributions exactly like any other; the risk is not de jure discounting but de facto reputational discounting by letter-writers and committees who read the Org Science editorial and view a sudden spike in output as a quality red flag.
- The documented AI-retraction mechanism to date is fabricated citations, not specification search; a pipeline that writes literature reviews with LLMs carries a concrete, already-prosecuted retraction risk, whereas model-selection practices remain unpoliced.

### Gaps
- Searches on AACSB Standard 8 + AI and on P&T committee AI research policies could not be run (search budget exhausted); no evidence found either way.
- No management or business-school retraction attributable to AI use was found; this is a null result from limited searching, not confirmation that none exist.
- Business-school letter-writer or committee attitudes toward AI-heavy CVs are undocumented in the sources found.
